## ⭐ the verdict, up front: can you insure a TCPA claim or not?

**yes — but not by product label, and this brief cannot tell you the price.** the body below is a
document procedure, so the answer is easy to assemble and easy to miss. it is stated here so no
reader has to.

| the question | the answer | the evidence |
|--------------|------------|--------------|
| **does cover for a TCPA claim exist at all?** | ✅ **yes.** two non-CGL forms answered a TCPA claim in litigated decisions, and a CGL's TCPA exclusion has been **negotiated out** by endorsement in a real policy | `US Bus Charter` · `Sempris` · `Yahoo` |
| **does the standard CGL cover it?** | ⛔ **no, by design.** the TCPA is named in the exclusion — see the paired hazard brief | `hazard.either-question-…` |
| **is a privacy-labelled product a safe substitute?** | ⛔ **no.** two privacy-adjacent products were tested and **both failed** — one cyber, one management-liability | `Bochenek` · `Horn` |
| **then what decides it?** | ⭐ the **grant's subject** (information vs contact) and the **exclusion set** — never the product's name. that is step 4 | step 4 · step 5 |
| **what does it cost? what does it cap at? who writes it?** | ⬜ **unknown — not researched.** no premium, no affirmative limit, and no carrier list is evidenced anywhere in this brief | the honest gap |

⚠️ **so read the title narrowly.** this brief answers *does the paper I hold — or am offered — reach a
TCPA claim, and what do I trade to change that*. it does **not** answer *where do I go buy it*. every
decision cited here is a dispute over an instrument the insured had **already bought**, which is why
the procurement half is a ⬜ gap rather than a thin section.

## ⛔ read this before the sequence: neither condition is curable after a claim

the hazard brief's two questions — does the **grant** reach the claim, and does an **exclusion** bar
it — share a property that decides everything about when this work is worth doing:

| the question | what settles it | when it can still be changed |
|--------------|-----------------|------------------------------|
| the grant | the **law of the state where suit lands** | ⛔ never, by you |
| the exclusion | **language bought years earlier** | only **before** the claim |

⭐ **so no amount of care in the campaign moves either one.** a flawless consent record is a defence
to the TCPA claim; it is not a defence to a coverage denial. these are separate fights with separate
documents, and the coverage fight is won or lost at purchase time.

## the sequence

### 1. obtain the policy — not the certificate

⛔ **a certificate of insurance cannot answer either question.** it shows that cover exists and what
the limits are; the endorsement schedule that decides whether *this claim* is covered is a **separate
artifact**.

the extant `counselor/briefs/howto.verify-gl-action-over-exclusion.[lesson].md` sets out that
distinction and the procedure to extract an endorsement list — **read it there; it is not restated
here.**

⚠️ **one difference from that brief is worth naming.** it is written for a party who must pry
documents out of *someone else's* carrier (a general contractor's). here the policy is **your own**,
so the leverage is inverted: you are the named insured and may simply demand the full policy and
every endorsement. what you lose is the excuse — a buyer who never read their own form cannot say
the document was withheld.

ask for three things, by name:

| artifact | why |
|----------|-----|
| the complete policy, not the declarations page | the grant language lives in the definitions |
| the **full endorsement schedule** | an endorsement can create *and* destroy cover — see step 5 |
| the **form edition stamp** | editions differ; see below |

### 2. read the edition stamp

`CG 00 01` is versioned, and the version is part of the answer. a seven-edition probe of published
opinions attested `10 01`, `12 04`, `12 07` and `04 13`; `04 13` is the latest attested and is still
named in opinions filed in 2024 and 2025.

⚠️ **that is a floor, not a currency claim.** ISO forms are licensed rather than published, so case
law is the only public index of them and it lags release by years. **and not every carrier writes on
ISO forms at all** — a surplus-lines or specialty form may carry no `CG` number whatsoever.

### 3. search the exclusion's **substance**, never its title

at least two different titles carry the same substance. a title search misses it. search the text for
the operative words instead — a clause that bars injury *"arising directly or indirectly out of any
action or omission that violates or is alleged to violate"* a named statute, with the TCPA in the
enumerated list.

### 4. ⭐ ask what the grant is keyed to — **information**, or **contact**

this is the single discriminator, and it explains every outcome below.

a TCPA claim is about the **act of the call**. it discloses no secret. so a grant keyed to *data*
does not reach it, however the product is labelled.

`Doctors Direct Ins., Inc. v. Bochenek` construes a real **cyber claims endorsement**:

> "Subject to all terms, conditions, definitions and exclusions of the Policy, we agree to reimburse
> protected parties, **up to the applicable limit indicated in this endorsement**, for costs
> protected parties become legally obligated to pay as a result of a Cyber Claim for any Network
> Security Wrongful Act or Privacy Wrongful Act … and for Data Recovery Costs incurred due to a Data
> Interference Act, including Defense Costs of a Government Investigation for a Privacy Wrongful
> Act."

the Illinois Appellate Court's disposition was **"Affirmed"** — cover denied. the hazard brief
carries the court's own reasons; they are not restated here.

**the test that follows:** read the grant's nouns. if they are *information*, *data*, *records*,
*personally identifiable*, the product is keyed to a breach and a TCPA claim falls outside it. this
is why "we have cyber" is not an answer to a robotext class action.

### 5. ⚠️ if you negotiate the exclusion out, read what replaces it

a buy-back is real. the Ninth Circuit's account of the policy in `Yahoo`, verbatim:

> "The standard policy excluded personal and advertising injury arising from the distribution of
> material in violation of the TCPA. The endorsement modified this coverage in three key ways.
> **First, it deleted the express exclusion of injuries** [arising from the distribution of material
> in violation of the TCPA]…"

⚠️ **read the count in that sentence: "three key ways."** the deletion was the *first* of three, and
a buyer who stops at the first has bought the exclusion's removal without pricing what came with it.
the California Supreme Court's summary of the net effect:

> "although Endorsement No. 1's coverage provision **created liability coverage for privacy
> injuries**, the same endorsement **expressly carved out** liability coverage for privacy injuries
> caused by material in a Yahoo! advertisement."

⭐ **that carve-out sits exactly where a promotional text lands**, and the court expressly declined to
decide whether the messages were advertisements under the policy.

**so the rule is: a buy-back is worth what its replacement text says, never what its deletion says.**
verify separately that no *other* clause undoes it — a broad "statute endorsement" elsewhere in the
same policy can neutralize a specific deletion, and no court in that line has settled whether it does.

## ⭐ the trap that no reflex catches: the same privacy words, on the other side of the policy

this is the finding most likely to reverse a buyer's intuition, and it does not appear in the hazard
brief.

in a CGL, **"right of privacy"** is part of the **grant** — the language that lets a TCPA claim *in*.
in a management-liability form it can be an **exclusion** — language that keeps the same claim *out*.

`Horn v. Liberty Ins. Underwriters, Inc.` (S.D. Fla. 2019) is the demonstration. the insured was
iCan, *"a national direct response marketer and seller of insurance products"*, and:

> "The Class Action asserted violations of the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, 47 U.S.C. § 227
> ('TCPA') against iCan."

the policy was a **"Private Advantage Insurance Policy"**, not a CGL. the carrier pleaded its
**Exclusion B.4, the "Invasion of Privacy Exclusion"**, which bars:

> "Loss on account of any Claim made against the Company … based upon, arising out of, or
> attributable to any actual or alleged … invasion of privacy[.]"

the insured's answer was that privacy was only one component and that *"they did not have to prove
invasion of privacy in order to prevail in the Class Action since it is not an element of the TCPA
cause of action."* **that argument lost.** the disposition:

> "Plaintiff's Motion for Summary Judgment [DE 108] is DENIED. Defendant's Motion for Summary
> Judgment [DE 113] is GRANTED."

⚠️ note what else the carrier had available in the same policy — each an independent bar a TCPA
claimant may run into:

| exclusion pleaded | why it bites a TCPA claim |
|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Invasion of Privacy** (B.4) | the ground the court took |
| **Unfair Trade Practices** (B.6) | a TCPA claim is often pleaded alongside a state UDAP count |
| **Willful Violation of Law** (A.6 / Endorsement 13) | ⭐ the TCPA **trebles** on a willful or knowing violation, so the plaintiff's own best theory can trigger the exclusion |
| **Professional Services** (B.3) | scope-of-work carve-out |

⭐ **the Willful Violation row is the sharpest.** the treble-damages theory that makes a TCPA claim
expensive is the same theory that can put it outside the policy. the worse the claim, the likelier
the exclusion.

**the practical instruction:** when you find a privacy word in a policy, do not stop at the word.
**find out which section it is in.** in the grant it is your friend; in the exclusions it is the
denial letter.

## defence costs — the question with the largest dollar consequence

on a per-message statute the defence spend on a class action can rival the indemnity. so **whether
defence costs sit inside or outside the limit** is often worth more than the limit's headline number.

policies say this plainly when they mean it. from the policy construed in `Southern Healthcare
Services, Inc. v. Lloyd's of London` (Miss. 2013), verbatim, in the form's own emphasis:

> "ERODE THE APPLICABLE LIMITS OF INSURANCE SHOWN IN THE DECLARATIONS. Expenses we incur in the
> investigating and defending claims and suits are included in the deductible."

with the Declarations Page marked *"Defense Costs included."* — and signed by the insured's own
representative. the Mississippi Supreme Court's disposition: **"We affirm the circuit court's grant
of summary judgment"** — the insureds lost.

⚠️ **the lesson is the signature.** the erosion term was on the Declarations Page, in capitals, and
acknowledged. a buyer who does not ask this question is not tricked; they are simply not read.

### what to put to a carrier or broker about any privacy-labelled product

| ask | why it matters |
|-----|----------------|
| is the grant keyed to **information** or to **contact**? | step 4 — the single discriminator |
| is the TCPA **named** anywhere, in a grant or an exclusion? | `Bochenek` and `Horn` cut opposite ways on privacy words |
| is there an **invasion of privacy exclusion**? | `Horn` — it defeats the claim a privacy product appears to cover |
| is there a **willful violation of law** exclusion? | the TCPA's treble theory may trigger it |
| are defence costs **inside or outside** the limit? | `Southern Healthcare` — erosion is enforced as written |
| is there a **sublimit** distinct from the policy limit? | `Bochenek`'s endorsement paid *"up to the applicable limit indicated in this endorsement"* |
| does the carrier owe a **duty to defend** or only to indemnify? | `Sempris`: *"if even one count or theory … lies within the coverage of the policy, the duty to defend arises"* |

## what a specialty form buys, and what is still unknown

two specialty (non-CGL) forms have answered a TCPA claim:

| decision | the form | outcome |
|----------|----------|---------|
| `Ill. Union Ins. Co. v. US Bus Charter & Limo Inc.` (E.D.N.Y. 2018) | professional liability, keyed to *"Professional Services"* | claimant won |
| `RSUI Indemnity Co. v. Sempris, LLC` (Del. Super. Ct. 2014) | excess/specialty; four bars pleaded | insured won a duty to defend |

⚠️ **but a specialty label settles none of this on its own.** `Bochenek` (cyber) and `Horn`
(management liability) are both privacy-adjacent products and both **failed**. what separated them
was the grant's subject and the exclusion set — step 4 and the section above — never the product's
name.

## the honest gap

- ⬜ **what such cover costs, and what it caps at, is unknown.** a six-facet sweep returned
  **NO-DECISION-FOUND** on the facet that asked for a decision that quotes an affirmative TCPA
  **limit or sublimit**. so this brief can tell a reader what to ask; it cannot tell them what a
  reasonable answer sounds like in dollars.
- ⬜ **how such a product is obtained — from whom, on what underwriting, at what premium — was not
  researched.** every decision here is a dispute about an instrument the insured had **already
  bought**. that is a document question, and this brief answers only document questions.
- ⚠️ **all seven decisions are single-state.** California, Illinois, Mississippi, Delaware, New York
  and Florida law appear here. the five-state sweep this repo normally applies has **not** been run
  on the coverage question, and coverage law is state law.
- ⚠️ **`Horn` and `Southern Healthcare` are each one policy.** they demonstrate that an
  invasion-of-privacy exclusion and a defence-erosion clause **exist and get enforced**; they do not
  establish how common either is.
- ⬜ **whether a carrier will actually agree to any of this** is a market fact, not a legal one, and
  no negotiation outcome is evidenced here.

| ⭐ **can a TCPA claim be insured at all?** | ✅ **yes** — two non-CGL forms answered one, and a CGL exclusion has been bought out by endorsement. ⛔ but **not** by a standard CGL, and ⛔ **not** reliably by a privacy label |
