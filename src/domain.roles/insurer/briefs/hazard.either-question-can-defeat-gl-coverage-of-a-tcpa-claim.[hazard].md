# hazard.either-question-can-defeat-gl-coverage-of-a-tcpa-claim

## .what

whether a commercial general liability (CGL) policy answers a claim under the Telephone Consumer
Protection Act turns on **two independent questions**, and a reflexive *"we carry GL, so we're
covered"* skips both:

1. **the grant** — does *"personal and advertising injury"* reach a TCPA claim at all? this is
   contested, splits by state, and turns on a distinction most readers have never met (privacy as
   **seclusion** vs privacy as **secrecy**)
2. **the exclusion** — standard forms carry an exclusion that names the TCPA in its first clause. it
   travels under **more than one title**, and it can be **negotiated away** — which introduces a
   third trap of its own

each question can defeat coverage on its own. an answer to one is not an answer to the other.

## .why

- the exclusion is easy to find once you know its name, and **the grant question is invisible** — a
  policy can be read cover-to-cover without a hint that "right of privacy" is contested
- the assumption that the TCPA exclusion is immovable is **wrong**, and the correction cuts both
  ways: a negotiated removal is attested in a reported case, and a buy-back can be silently undone
  elsewhere in the same policy
- TCPA damages are per-message and aggregate as a class, so the coverage answer is load-bearing at a
  scale where a wrong assumption is not survivable by cash flow

> **not legal advice.** informational groundwork for counsel and a broker to validate. coverage turns
> on the specific policy, its endorsements, and the law of the governing state.

## question 1 — the grant: does "right of privacy" reach a TCPA claim?

many CGL policies cover injury *"arising out of … [o]ral or written publication, in any manner, of
material that violates a person's right of privacy."* whether that language reaches a TCPA claim
depends on which **kind** of privacy it means.

the distinction, as the California Supreme Court quoted it:

> "[A] person claiming the privacy right of **seclusion** asserts the right to be free, in a
> particular location, from disturbance by others. A person claiming the privacy right of **secrecy**
> asserts the right to prevent disclosure of personal information to others. Invasion of the privacy
> right of seclusion involves the means, manner, and method of communication in a location (or at a
> time) which disturbs the recipient's seclusion. By contrast, invasion of the privacy right of
> secrecy involves the content of communication…"

a TCPA claim is a **seclusion** claim — an unwanted robotext discloses no secret. so if the grant
reaches only secrecy, a TCPA claim never enters the policy at all, and the exclusion is never even
reached.

### the split is real, and it is by state

the Ninth Circuit catalogued the division rather than guess at it, and **certified the question to
the California Supreme Court** rather than decide it:

> "Courts nationwide are divided as to whether such a provision covers injury solely to the right to
> seclusion…"

with coverage found in Missouri, Florida and Massachusetts decisions, and no coverage found by the
Seventh Circuit under Iowa law and the Fourth Circuit under Virginia law — and **two California Court
of Appeal decisions in tension with each other**. the panel wrote that although it "may hazard a
guess," that tension "inhibit[s] our ability to 'predict how the'" state court would rule.

| jurisdiction posture | effect on a TCPA claim |
|----------------------|------------------------|
| grant reaches **seclusion** | the claim enters the policy; the exclusion then decides it |
| grant reaches **secrecy only** | the claim never enters the policy; the exclusion is moot |

**sources**: [Yahoo! Inc. v. National Union, 913 F.3d 923 (9th Cir. 2019) — order that certifies the question](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4582011/yahoo-inc-v-national-union-fire-insurance/) ·
[State Farm Gen. Ins. Co. v. JT's Frames, Inc., 181 Cal.App.4th 429 (2010)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2255989/state-farm-general-insurance-v-jts-frames-inc/)

### how California answered

`Yahoo Inc. v. National Union Fire Ins. Co. of Pittsburgh, PA`, No. S253593 (Cal. Nov. 17, 2022),
Jenkins, J., for a unanimous court:

> "The question we must resolve is whether this language provides liability coverage for
> right-of-seclusion violations litigated under the TCPA. **We conclude that it does, assuming such
> coverage is consistent with the insured's reasonable expectations.**"

and its own statement of the limits, verbatim from the closing paragraph:

> "…can cover liability for violations of the right of seclusion **if such coverage is consistent
> with the insured's objectively reasonable expectations**. Such a policy can also trigger the
> insurer's duty to defend the insured against a claim that the insured violated the TCPA by sending
> unsolicited text messages that did not reveal any private or secret information, **provided that
> the alleged TCPA violation amounts to a right-of-seclusion violation under California law**. The
> fact that such a policy has been modified by an endorsement with regard to advertising injuries may
> affect such coverage and such duty to defend, **but we have no occasion to decide that issue
> here.**"

⚠️ **this is a conditional answer, not a green light.** it is California law; it depends on the
insured's objectively reasonable expectations, which the court said "must be determined in further
litigation"; and it expressly leaves the endorsement question open.

**source**: [Yahoo Inc. v. National Union, No. S253593 (Cal. 2022)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8512158/yahoo-inc-v-nat-union-fire-ins-co-of-pittsburgh-pa/)

## question 2 — the exclusion: named, but not fixed

### it names the TCPA first

the ISO Commercial General Liability Coverage Form `CG 00 01 04 13` carries, under **both** Coverage
A and Coverage B, an exclusion titled *"Recording And Distribution Of Material Or Information In
Violation Of Law"*, which excludes injury —

> "arising directly or indirectly out of any action or omission that violates or is alleged to
> violate: (1) The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), including any amendment of or addition
> to such law;"

— followed by CAN-SPAM, FCRA/FACTA, and a catch-all for any other statute that "addresses, prohibits,
or limits the printing, dissemination, disposal, collecting, recording, sending, transmitting,
communicating or distribution of material or information."

⭐ **that text is independently attested by a court.** rather than rest on a posted specimen alone,
note that the Maryland Court of Special Appeals reproduced the same exclusion, by the same title,
from the policies before it — three statutes named (TCPA, CAN-SPAM, FCRA) plus the same catch-all.
a court's reproduction of the policy in the record is a stronger attestation of the form's language
than a specimen posted by a third party.

### ⚠️ it travels under more than one title

a policy may carry the same substance under a different heading. in one reported case the insurer's
denial letter cited the *"RECORDING AND DISTRIBUTION OF MATERIAL OR INFORMATION IN VIOLATION OF LAW
EXCLUSION"*, while the policy provision itself was headed **"Distribution of Material in Violation Of
Statutes"** — and its text reached the TCPA, CAN-SPAM, and any other statute that "prohibits or
limits the sending, transmitting, communicating or distribution of material or information."

**so a policy search keyed to one title can miss the exclusion.** the substance travels under at
least two titles — a clause that names the TCPA, or one that reaches statutes about the transmission
of material — and the title is not a reliable index to it.

**source**: [Bullseye Rest., Inc. v. James River Ins. Co., 387 F. Supp. 3d 273 (E.D.N.Y. 2019)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7336509/bullseye-rest-inc-v-james-river-ins-co/)

### ⚠️ editions differ, so the edition stamp is part of the answer

the form is versioned, and different policy years carry different editions. one court described
policies written on **`CG 00 01 12 04`** and **`CG 00 01 12 07`** in consecutive years for the same
insured, and noted that a relevant exclusion appeared in one policy year and not the earlier ones.
the edition stamp on the form is part of the answer.

### ⭐ the exclusion can be bought back — and the buy-back can be undone

the assumption that this exclusion is immovable is wrong. the Yahoo! policy was National Union's
standard CGL, and the standard form "excluded injuries arising from the distribution of material in
violation of the TCPA" — but a **negotiated endorsement removed that exclusion**. the whole coverage
dispute exists because the TCPA exclusion had been bargained away.

⚠️ **and the trap that came with it.** a *separate* endorsement in the same policy — a "Statute
Endorsement" — added an exclusion for liability arising from:

> "any act that violates any statute … of any federal [or] state … government, … that … addresses or
> applies to the sending, transmitting or communicating of any material or information, by any means
> whatsoever."

the Ninth Circuit asked whether that generic clause made the removal of the specific TCPA exclusion
"without substantive effect." the California Supreme Court's answer was:

> "We express no view on the question."

**so a buy-back of the named TCPA exclusion may be neutralized by a broader statutory exclusion
elsewhere in the same policy — and no court in this line has settled it.**

---

## the exclusion is not unlimited

the exclusion's catch-all is broad, but a court has declined to read it as a general bar. in the
Maryland case, on a claim about unauthorized video surveillance in a restroom — **not** a TCPA claim —
the court held:

> "We also conclude that an exclusion for 'Recording and Distribution of Material or Information in
> Violation of Law' does not preclude coverage."

⚠️ **do not over-read this.** it is a non-TCPA claim, so it says an insurer cannot stretch the
catch-all to any recording-adjacent tort; it does not speak to whether the exclusion bars a claim
brought under the statute the exclusion names first.

**source**: [Harleysville Preferred Ins. Co. v. Rams Head Savage Mill, LLC, 237 Md. App. 705 (2018)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4512318/harleysville-preferred-ins-co-v-rams-head-savage-mill-llc/)

## what the exposure looks like when coverage fails

a junk-fax TCPA class action over "tens of thousands" of unsolicited faxes settled for
**$19,520,000**, with the settlement enforceable only against the defendant's insurance proceeds and
the defendant's coverage rights assigned to the class. the insurer then won a declaration of **no
coverage** on the grant question — the policy's "right of privacy" language was read not to reach
seclusion.

that is the shape of the downside: the settlement is structured to reach the policy, and then the
policy is held not to answer.

**source**: [State Farm Gen. Ins. Co. v. JT's Frames, Inc., 181 Cal.App.4th 429 (2010)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2255989/state-farm-general-insurance-v-jts-frames-inc/)

## ⭐ the next reflex fails too — a cyber endorsement is not a TCPA backstop

once "we have GL" fails, the natural next move is to reach for an adjacent product already on the
policy. a survey run for this brief found that move fails for a **third**, independent reason — and
the reason is not an exclusion at all.

`Doctors Direct Ins., Inc. v. Bochenek`, 2015 IL App (1st) 142919, construes a real **cyber claims
endorsement** against a real TCPA text-message class action. the endorsement's grant, verbatim:

> "Subject to all terms, conditions, definitions and exclusions of the Policy, we agree to reimburse
> protected parties, up to the applicable limit indicated in this endorsement, for costs protected
> parties become legally obligated to pay as a result of a Cyber Claim for any Network Security
> Wrongful Act or Privacy Wrongful Act including Patient Notification Costs and Credit Monitoring
> Costs incurred for any Privacy Wrongful Act and for Data Recovery Costs incurred due to a Data
> Interference Act…"

on its face that reads like it should answer an unsolicited-text claim: it is a **privacy** grant,
and a TCPA claim is a privacy claim. it did not. the court's rationale, verbatim:

> "Here, Bochenek's complaints alleged a violation of the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, but
> because this statute is not associated with the control and use of personally identifiable
> financial, credit or medical information, the allegation is not covered by…"

and the conclusion:

> "Overall, because the allegations in Bochenek's federal complaints do not even potentially fall
> within the coverage of the Doctors Direct policy, Doctors Direct does not have a duty to defend or
> indemnify McAdoo in the federal lawsuit."

disposition: **"Affirmed."**

⭐ **the mechanism deserves its own name, because it is not either of the two above.** the CGL
failures are a **grant** dispute (does "right of privacy" reach seclusion?) and an **exclusion**
dispute (does a named clause bar it?). this is neither. the cyber grant was keyed to
**information** — "personally identifiable financial, credit, or medical information" — and a TCPA
claim is about the **act of the call**, not about information. so the claim fell outside the grant
on a definitional axis the policy never advertised as a limit.

**the generalizable point: a privacy-labeled product is not a TCPA product.** whether a given policy
answers turns on what its privacy grant is keyed to, and a cyber form is keyed to data, not to
contact.

⚠️ **scope.** one intermediate state appellate decision, on one carrier's endorsement text. it does
**not** establish that cyber products generally exclude TCPA claims, and a cyber or media form
drafted differently may reach one. what it establishes is that the reflex is not safe, and that the
operative distinction is **what the grant is keyed to — information, or contact.**

**source**: [Doctors Direct Ins., Inc. v. Bochenek, 2015 IL App (1st) 142919](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/3003491/doctors-direct-insurance-inc-v-bochenek/)

## ⭐ what the buy-back endorsement actually traded

> ⚠️ **this section corrects a self-contradiction in an earlier version of this brief.** the section
> *"the exclusion can be bought back"* above already recorded that a negotiated endorsement removed
> the TCPA exclusion — while the survey table just above this one stated that "no decision that
> construes an affirmative TCPA grant was located." **both claims sat in the same document, and they
> cannot both be true.** the defect was never an absent source; it was that the two halves of the
> brief were never read against each other. the correction is stated rather than quietly patched, and
> the class of defect is exactly what a brief-integrity check exists to catch.

the section above establishes **that** the exclusion was bought back. this one establishes **what the
buy-back cost**, which the earlier read never asked — and the answer changes how a buy-back should be
valued.

### ⚠️ the buy-back was a trade, not a gift

Endorsement No. 1 made **three** changes, not one. the deletion of the TCPA exclusion was only the
first. the California Supreme Court's account of the other two:

> "…it narrowed the definition of covered injuries to 'personal injury' (as compared to 'personal and
> advertising injury' in the standard version of the policy), and it defined 'personal injury' to
> include injury arising from any of **five** offenses (as compared to **seven** offenses in the
> standard version)."

> "Third, and related to the second change, Endorsement No. 1 **expressly excluded** liability
> coverage for 'advertising injury'…"

and the court's own summary of the net effect:

> "although Endorsement No. 1's coverage provision **created liability coverage for privacy
> injuries**, the same endorsement **expressly carved out** liability coverage for privacy injuries
> caused by material in a Yahoo! advertisement."

⭐ **that carve-out sits exactly where a marketing text lands.** a promotional message is the most
likely message to be called an "advertisement", so a buy-back written this way may hand back the very
claim it appeared to cover. and the court **declined to say** whether Yahoo's texts were
advertisements under the policy:

> "our statement of the certified question should not be interpreted to express this court's opinion
> as to whether the text messages at issue in the underlying TCPA lawsuits were, in fact,
> advertisements as defined in the policy."

**what these two decisions establish:** a buy-back's effect is fixed by its *replacement* text, not
by its deletion. the struck clause is only half the endorsement, and the half the courts construed
was the other one.

### two specialty forms that did respond

| decision | the instrument | disposition |
|----------|----------------|-------------|
| `Ill. Union Ins. Co. v. US Bus Charter & Limo Inc.` (E.D.N.Y. 2018) | a **professional liability** form that covers a *"Personal Injury Offense committed by [the insured] … in the performance of … Professional Services"*, where Professional Services meant *"[s]olely in the performance of professional services as a bus charter broker for others for a fee"* | ☀️ **claimant won.** *"[the claimant's] motion for partial summary judgment is GRANTED, and Illinois Union's motion for partial summary judgment is DENIED."* |
| `RSUI Indemnity Co. v. Sempris, LLC` (Del. Super. Ct. 2014) | an excess/specialty form; the insurer pleaded four independent bars, among them *"Section IV., Exclusions, Paragraph 11"* and *"The Endorsement Titled 'Exclusion – Specific'"* | ☀️ **insured won.** *"RSUI's Motion for Summary Judgment is hereby DENIED. Sempris' Motion for Partial Summary Judgment is hereby GRANTED."* — a duty to defend the underlying TCPA class action |

`Sempris` also records the defense-side rule that makes these fights lopsided in the insured's favor
once the claim is inside the policy at all:

> "if even one count or theory of plaintiff's complaint lies within the coverage of the policy, the
> duty to defend arises."

⚠️ **both are trial-level and state-specific** (New York and Delaware law respectively). neither is a
market survey, and neither tells a reader what such a form costs or caps.

## the two questions, side by side

| | question 1 — the grant | question 2 — the exclusion |
|---|------------------------|----------------------------|
| **what it asks** | does "right of privacy" reach seclusion? | does a clause bar statute-based claims? |
| **where to look** | the definition of "personal and advertising injury" | exclusions in Coverage A **and** Coverage B, plus every endorsement |
| **what varies** | **state law** — genuinely split | **the policy** — title, edition, endorsements |
| **can it be negotiated?** | not readily — it is the standard grant language | **yes** — the named TCPA exclusion is bought back in practice |
| **the trap** | invisible; the policy gives no hint the term is contested | a buy-back may be undone by a broader statute endorsement |

## severity

**conditional** — **outside where the governing state reads the grant to reach seclusion and the
policy carries no statute-based exclusion**, **blocker if either condition fails**.

the two conditions are independent and each is enough on its own. the grant condition turns on
**state law**, which is genuinely split — coverage found in Missouri, Florida and Massachusetts
decisions, no coverage found by the Seventh Circuit under Iowa law and the Fourth under Virginia,
and two California Court of Appeal decisions in tension until the state supreme court answered. the
exclusion condition turns on **the specific policy** — its title, its edition, and every endorsement.

the downside is not a coverage haircut; it is the whole loss. a junk-fax TCPA class action over
"tens of thousands" of unsolicited faxes settled for **$19,520,000**, structured to reach the
policy — and the insurer then won a declaration of **no coverage** on the grant question alone. the
exclusion was never reached.

⚠️ **and neither condition is curable by disclosure or by care in the campaign.** each is decided by
the law of the served state and by policy language bought years earlier, so both can only be
addressed **before** a claim, with a broker.

## the honest gap

- 🔶 **the affirmative market was surveyed twice, and the gap narrowed on the second pass.** the first
  survey ran two caselaw queries and concluded that no decision construes an affirmative TCPA grant.
  **that conclusion was wrong** — a widened six-facet sweep found four decisions, and one of them
  construes an endorsement that **deleted the TCPA exclusion outright**. ⚠️ the correction matters
  beyond its content: the earlier null was produced by **two queries**, and two queries is one attempt,
  not a search. a null from a narrow sweep is not evidence of absence.
- ⬜ **what such a policy is worth in dollars is still unknown.** the sweep's `endorsement-sublimit`
  facet returned **NO-DECISION-FOUND** — no decision read here quotes a limit or a sublimit on TCPA
  cover. the open-web route produced only **vendor and trade pages**, which under
  `rule.require.bhrowser-citations` are locators, not authority. so the market is proven to **exist**
  and proven to have **answered twice**; its **price and its cap remain unresearched**.
- ⚠️ **the four decisions read are not a survey of the affirmative market.** two are trial-level
  (E.D.N.Y. and Del. Super.), each on one state's law, and all four are disputes about instruments the
  insureds had **already bought**. how such an instrument is obtained, from whom, or on what terms was
  not researched — that is a market question and this brief answers only the document question.
- 🔶 **`CG 00 01 04 13` is the latest edition attested in case law — which is a floor, not proof of
  currency.** a seven-edition probe of published opinions attested `10 01`, `12 04`, `12 07` and
  `04 13`, and returned **zero** for three speculative later designations (`04 17`, `12 19`, `04 24`).
  `04 13` is still named in opinions filed in **2024 and 2025**, so it is demonstrably in live use.
  ⚠️ **but ISO forms are licensed, not published, so case law is the only public index and it lags
  form release by years.** an edition absent from this probe may exist and simply not have been
  litigated yet. the honest claim is *"the latest edition this research can evidence"* — never
  *"the current edition"*. **and not every carrier writes on ISO forms at all**, so even a correct
  currency answer would not tell a reader what is on their own policy.
- **no survey of state law on the grant question was run.** the split is documented from one court's
  catalogue; only California's answer was read in full. the five-state sweep this repo normally
  requires has **not** been run on this question.
- **no decision was found that applies the exclusion to bar a TCPA claim.** the exclusion's text names
  the TCPA plainly, and no case read here holds it inapplicable to one — but that a court has enforced
  it *against a TCPA claim* is **not** established by the sources here, and should not be assumed from
  the text alone.
- **the endorsement question is open by name.** the California Supreme Court expressly declined it.

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| is "we have GL" an answer? | no — it addresses neither the grant question nor the exclusion |
| what is the grant question? | whether "publication of material that violates a person's right of privacy" reaches **seclusion**; a TCPA claim is a seclusion claim |
| is the grant question settled? | no — split by state; California answered yes, **conditioned** on the insured's objectively reasonable expectations |
| does the standard form exclude the TCPA? | it names the TCPA in the first clause of an exclusion that sits under **both** coverage parts |
| is the exclusion identifiable by its title? | ⚠️ not reliably — at least two distinct titles carry the same substance, so the title is not a stable handle on it |
| can the exclusion be removed? | **yes** — a named endorsement that **deleted** it is quoted by two courts |
| does removal settle it? | ⚠️ **no, and the reason is sharper than "maybe".** the same endorsement that deleted the TCPA exclusion **carved out privacy injury caused by material in an advertisement** — which is where a marketing text most likely lands |
| what fixes a buy-back's effect? | ⭐ **its replacement text, not its deletion.** in both decisions what the endorsement put back decided the claim; what it struck only opened the door |
| what determines the answer for a given policy? | the grant language, every endorsement, the form edition, and the governing state — the four variables the analysis above turns on |
| **is a cyber endorsement the fallback?** | ⭐ **not reliably.** one decision construes a real cyber "privacy wrongful act" grant and holds it does **not** reach a TCPA claim — because the grant is keyed to **information**, and a TCPA claim is about the **act of the call** |
| what distinguishes a privacy-labeled product that answers from one that does not? | **what its grant is keyed to — information, or contact.** a form keyed to data does not reach a contact claim |
| is there an affirmative TCPA market? | 🔶 **it exists and it has answered — its price is unknown.** an endorsement that deleted the TCPA exclusion is quoted by two courts, and two specialty forms owed a defense. ⬜ **no decision quotes a limit or sublimit**, so what such cover is worth in dollars is unresearched |
| does a specialty form beat a CGL here? | ⚠️ **not by its label.** `Bochenek` (cyber) failed; `Ill. Union` (professional liability) and `RSUI` (excess) answered. what decided each was **the grant's subject**, never the product's name |
| **where are the endorsements actually recorded?** | ⭐ **not on a certificate of insurance.** the endorsement list is a separate artifact from the certificate, and the extant `counselor/briefs/howto.verify-gl-action-over-exclusion.[lesson].md` covers that distinction; this brief does not restate it |
