# define.float-interest-on-debt-reserve

## .what

a party may hold cash that offsets an outstanding points-liability. the legal question: **on which side of
the title line does that cash sit, and where does the law place the yield?**

the answer turns on one prior fact: whether a reward point is **a debt a party owes** (earned, not
deposited) or **deposited / stored value**.

- where the point is a **debt** a party owes → the offset cash is that party's **own general capital**, the
  same as any business that carries accounts-payable. the law imposes no duty to segregate and no duty to
  pass interest through; yield follows legal title to the funds.
- where the point is **deposited / stored value** (a person's funds a party holds in custody) → the cash
  is the customer's money → stored-value / money-transmitter regimes restrict it: permissible-investments
  limits, segregation, no commingle, and the yield may be regulated.

the rule is symmetric and neutral: **yield follows legal title to the funds.** a party that holds funds as
its own may retain the yield; a party that holds them in custody may not. this brief states where the line
sits — it does not conclude which side any actor captures.

## .why

- a debt is the debtor's **own** money with a payable that offsets; a deposit is **someone else's** money
  the holder merely custodies
- interest is earned by whoever **owns** the invested principal — so the debt-vs-deposit line also
  decides who owns the float and its yield
- which side a balance falls on is decided by whether value was earned or deposited; the two sides carry
  opposite investment and yield treatment

> **not legal advice. not tax / accounting advice.** informational groundwork for counsel and a CPA to
> validate.

## the two-row answer

| the point is… | whose money is the offset cash? | may it be invested? | who keeps the yield? | duties on the cash |
|---------------|---------------------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|--------------------|
| **a DEBT the issuer owes** (earned, not deposited) | the issuer's **own** general capital, with an offset payable | **yes** — like any accounts-payable carrier | **the issuer** — interest is the owner's income | none special: no segregation, no commingle bar, no pass-through |
| **DEPOSITED / stored value** (a person's funds, held) | the **customer's** money, the issuer is custodian | **restricted** — permissible-investments limits only | **regulated** — often owed to or protected for the customer | segregate, hold a match, no commingle |

the whole question collapses to the debt-vs-deposit line. an earned-not-deposited point sits on the top
row (investable general funds, yield with the title-holder); a deposited balance sits on the bottom row
(restricted custodial, yield with the funds' owner). the two rows are stated symmetrically; the law places
the yield with legal title on either side.

---

## side A — the point is a debt → the cash is the issuer's own investable capital

### a debt is money the company owes, from its own resources

cornell's legal dictionary states the shape plainly — a debt is an obligation the **debtor** owes a
creditor, not the creditor's money the debtor holds:

> "Debt is a financial liability or obligation owed by one person, the debtor, to another, the
> creditor."

**source**: [debt — Cornell LII (Wex)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/debt)

an accounts-payable is the everyday form of that debt — a company's **own** short-term debt to those
it owes:

> "Accounts payable is an accounting term that describes the short-term debt that a company owes to its
> suppliers or vendors for products or services received before a payment is made"

**source**: [accounts payable — Cornell LII (Wex)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/accounts_payable)

and a payable is booked as the company's **own** current liability on its balance sheet — not as a
custody balance of someone else's funds:

> "Accounts payable entries are a type of current liability in the business's general ledger and
> balance sheet"

**source**: [accounts payable — Cornell LII (Wex)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/accounts_payable)

the reward point sits in this family. cornell lists the peers:

> "Current liabilities are financial obligations generally due within one year. Examples of current
> liabilities are accounts payable, short-term debt, dividends, and income tax."

**source**: [current liability — Cornell LII (Wex)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/current_liability)

### the books treat an own-obligation point as the issuer's own liability

under ASC 606 a reward point is booked as the issuer's **own** liability (equal to deferred
revenue), recognized on redemption — never as a custody pot of a customer's cash:

> "With the introduction of Accounting Standards Codification ASC 606, the liability incurred by
> rewards program is becoming an increasingly important topic."

> "Rewards program liability is also equal to the deferred revenue."

> "The revenue gets recognized when points are redeemed."

and the unredeemed slice (breakage) — the portion of points a holder never redeems — is a defined ASC 606
concept, recognized as revenue once redemption is remote (or in proportion to rights exercised), not held
as a customer refund:

> "Breakage is the percentage of unredeemed points (either issued or expired)."

**source**: [financial accounting for liability from rewards programs (ASC 606) — TrueLoyal](https://www.trueloyal.com/blog/financial-accounting-for-liability-from-rewards-programs)

because the liability is the debtor's own, the cash set aside to meet that liability is likewise the
debtor's own. the law imposes on a company that carries accounts-payable no duty to fence off cash for its
vendors, nor to pass its bank interest through to them. an own-obligation points reserve is the same
species of cash — the same freedoms and the same absence of custodial duty attach as a matter of law.

### an earned-debt analogue — an accrued affiliate commission

the amazon associates program treats an earned-but-unpaid commission as an ordinary **payment
obligation** — the debtor's own liability that survives even termination:

> "the rights and obligations of the parties under Sections 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, and 11 of this
> Agreement and as specified in the Program Policies, together with any payable but unpaid payment
> obligations under this Agreement, will survive the termination"

**source**: [Amazon Associates Program Operating Agreement](https://affiliate-program.amazon.com/help/operating/agreement)

no one imagines amazon must fence off cash for accrued commissions or hand affiliates the interest its
cash earns before payout. an own-obligation points reserve carries the same freedom.

### the interest is the owner's income

federal tax law settles who the yield belongs to: interest is gross income of the party who earns it —
the owner of the invested principal:

> "Except as otherwise provided in this subtitle, gross income means all income from whatever source
> derived, including (but not limited to) the following items:"

and item (4) of the enumerated list is exactly:

> "Interest"

**source**: [26 U.S.C. § 61 — gross income](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/61)

where a party owns the offset cash (it is a debt, not a deposit), the interest earned on that cash
is **that party's** gross income — reported and taxed as such. there is no customer whose income it could
be, because no customer deposited the funds. the mirror also holds: where a customer did deposit the funds,
the yield is the customer's, not the holder's. the law places the yield with title on either side.

## side B — where the point is deposited value → the cash is restricted custodial money

flip the fact: where a person **loaded** funds and an issuer merely held them, the cash is the
customer's, and the regime changes.

### the funds are a customer's, not the issuer's

FinCEN's prepaid-access definition keys on **funds paid in advance** — value the holder funded, which
the issuer holds:

> "Access to funds or the value of funds that have been paid in advance and can be retrieved or
> transferred at some point in the future through an electronic device or vehicle, such as a card,
> code, electronic serial number, mobile identification number, or personal identification number"

**source**: [31 CFR § 1010.100(ww)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)

the Reg E prepaid-account definition keys on value **loaded** — again the holder's own funds:

> "issued on a prepaid basis in a specified amount or not issued on a prepaid basis but capable of
> being loaded with funds thereafter"

**source**: [12 CFR § 1005.2(b)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1005.2)

florida's definitions describe the same held value — "stored value" is monetary value **represented
and retrievable** as a medium of exchange:

> "'Stored value' means currency or monetary value represented in digital electronic format,
> regardless of whether it is specially encrypted, and stored or capable of storage on electronic
> media in such a way as to be retrievable and transferred electronically."

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 560.103(35)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/560.103)

### held funds get segregation + permissible-investments limits, not free investment

when the value is the customer's, state money-services law caps how it may be invested — the licensee
must at all times hold assets that match the outstanding obligation:

> "A licensee must at all times possess permissible investments with an aggregate market value,
> calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles, of at least the aggregate
> face amount of all outstanding money transmissions and payment instruments issued or sold by the
> licensee or an authorized vendor in the United States."

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 560.210(1)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/560.210)

for value-denominated obligations the match is even stricter — the transmitter must hold value of the
**same type and amount** owed, a near-total segregation duty:

> "Each money transmitter that receives virtual currency, either directly or through an authorized
> vendor, for the purpose of transmitting such virtual currency from one person to another location or
> person must at all times, until the transmission obligation is completed, hold virtual currency of
> the same type and amount owed or obligated to the other location or person."

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 560.210(2)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/560.210)

### custodial funds stay the underlying owner's, even in the bank

when funds are held for others, deposit-insurance law treats them as the **beneficial owner's** money,
not the holder's — the clearest statement that custody does not transfer title:

> ""Pass-through" deposit insurance is a method of insuring depositors whose funds are placed and held
> at an FDIC-insured bank through a third party."

> "The underlying owners, rather than the third party that maintains the account at the insured bank,
> actually own the funds."

**source**: [Your Insured Deposits — FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits/)

on this side the cash is not the issuer's to invest freely, and the yield is not automatically the issuer's —
it belongs to, or is protected for, the owner of the funds. this is the whole reason the debt-vs-
deposit line matters to the float question.

## what places a point on side A

a point that is **never bought, loaded, or deposited** — one **earned** for a contribution — brings no
"funds paid in advance" and no value "loaded" into the issuer's hands. FinCEN's own service definition
needs an **acceptance** of another's funds, which does not occur for an earned point:

> "acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the
> transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another location or
> person by any means"

**source**: [31 CFR § 1010.100(ff)(5)(i)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)

with no acceptance and no load, there is no customer deposit. the offset cash is the holder's own general
capital, with a payable — side A. as a matter of law it may be invested, and any interest is the
title-holder's income; conversely a load or deposit tips the balance to side B, where the yield follows the
depositor's title. the brief states where the line falls, not which side an actor elects.

## the honest gap

- this is a text-level read, strong but CPA- and counsel-confirmable; no decision addresses any specific
  earned-debt reserve.
- the debt characterization is decisive. where a design lets a person **load / buy / deposit** value, the
  reserve tips to side B and the segregation + permissible-investments duties attach. an earned-only,
  non-transferable, never-cash consumer path keeps a balance on side A; a load/deposit feature tips it to
  side B.
- state law is not uniform. the FL permissible-investments text is representative of the money-services
  regime, not a nationwide rule; per-state confirmation is required if the deposit side is ever in play.
- side A settles a **right** to invest, not a duty — an issuer on side A **may** invest the reserve.

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| may an own-obligation points reserve be invested? | as a matter of law, yes — it is the debtor's own general capital (a debt, not a deposit) |
| where does the interest / yield land? | with legal title — the owner's gross income (26 U.S.C. § 61); on the debt side the title-holder, on the deposit side the depositor |
| must a party segregate the cash? | on the debt side, no — a payable carrier does not fence off cash for what it owes; on the deposit side, yes |
| what changes the answer? | where points are deposited / loaded value → custodial, segregation + investment limits |
| the element that decides it? | whose money is it — a debt a party owes, or funds a person deposited |
| what places a balance on side A? | points are earned, never loaded or bought → side A |
