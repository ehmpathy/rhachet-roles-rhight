# hazard.escheat-dormant-prepaid-deposits

## .what

unclaimed-property (escheat) law can force a holder to hand a dormant balance to the state. the hazard
here is **narrow and distinct** from the points hazard: do **dormant, unspent prepaid BALANCES** escheat?

a prepaid balance is money a customer **paid** a seller up front for a future good or service — direct
monetary consideration for a good or service not yet delivered (a prepaid gift card, prepaid airline
miles, a funded ad account, a topped-up phone balance are the everyday forms). that paid-for character is
the whole hazard:

- a **reward POINT** is given for free (no consideration) → it fits the RUUPA **loyalty-card exclusion**
  and generally does **not** escheat.
- a **prepaid BALANCE** is **paid for** (direct monetary consideration) → it does **NOT** get the
  loyalty-card exclusion → a purchased, unspent, dormant balance is closer to **escheatable unclaimed
  property** (a credit balance / customer overpayment / stored value) than an earned point is.

so a dormant prepaid balance is the **exposed** balance, and that exposure **constrains** "breakage →
take" — a holder cannot keep an unspent balance as its own revenue where the state has first claim
on it.

## .why

- a prepaid balance is a paid-for obligation, so the loyalty-card exclusion (which turns on **no**
  consideration) does not reach it — the exclusion available to a free point is unavailable for a paid-for balance
- where a dormant balance escheats, the state, not the holder, takes it → "breakage → take" is **capped** by the
  escheat claim
- a **closed-loop merchant credit** (redeemable only for the seller's own good or service, never cash) sits on softer
  ground than a cash-refundable balance — the refund design is the lever
- states reach a paid-for balance differently (a per-state check is required), and diverge from how
  they treat a free point

## the consideration line — a paid-for balance is NOT a loyalty card

RUUPA draws its escheat line on **consideration**. a **loyalty card** is given for **free** and cannot
be monetized; a **stored-value card** is a promise backed **by consideration**. the D.C. enactment
states both verbatim:

> "A record given without direct monetary consideration under an award, reward, benefit, loyalty,
> incentive, rebate, or promotional program that may be used or redeemed only to obtain goods or
> services or a discount on goods or services. The term does not include a record that may be redeemed
> for money or otherwise monetized by the issuer."

> "A record evidencing a promise made for consideration by the seller or issuer of the record that
> goods, services, or money will be provided to the owner of the record to the value or amount shown in
> the record."

**source**: [D.C. Code § 41-151.02 — RUUPA loyalty-card vs stored-value definitions](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/41-151.02)

a paid-for prepaid balance is the **paid-for** side of that line — a customer handed a seller money for a
future good or service. it is not "given without direct monetary consideration," so the loyalty-card
exclusion does not reach it. a compliance analysis frames the same split — the free, no-consideration
profile is what earns the exclusion:

> "Balances cannot be converted to cash, cannot be converted to gift cards and must be used towards
> merchandise"

> "Customers did not pay for the points and no consideration was exchanged"

and it names the paid-for balances that **do** land in escheat exposure:

> "Customer deposits and refunds can become unclaimed property if customers do not claim them within a
> certain period"

**source**: [Sovos — are rebates, refunds and incentives considered unclaimed property?](https://sovos.com/blog/trr/are-rebates-refunds-and-incentives-considered-unclaimed-property/)

the balance was **paid for** → no loyalty-card exclusion → it reads as a customer deposit / credit
balance that can escheat.

## a purchased balance is a real owned obligation

federal law confirms a paid-for balance is a genuine, owned obligation — not a mere discount. gross
income is broad, and money paid for a service is a real claim on both sides of the ledger:

> "Gross income means all income from whatever source derived, including (but not limited to) the
> following items:"

with the first enumerated class:

> "Compensation for services, including fees, commissions, fringe benefits, and similar items"

**source**: [26 U.S.C. § 61 — gross income](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/61)

a customer who prepaid a balance holds a real, claimable interest in the unspent portion — exactly the
profile a state unclaimed-property administrator reaches for when the owner lets it sit dormant.

---

## the breakage-take constraint

"breakage → take" is the move where a seller recognizes an unredeemed / forfeited balance as its **own**
revenue. escheat **caps** that move. where the dormant balance must be remitted to the state, the seller never
gets to keep it — the state's claim comes first. the ASC 606 breakage guidance states the override
plainly:

> "If breakage amounts will be owed to the state, then a company should follow the normal process of
> accounting for breakage amounts with one major difference: when the entity would normally recognize
> revenue, a liability for breakage amounts payable to the government should be recognized instead."

**source**: [Unexercised Rights (Breakage) in ASC 606 — RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/unexercised-rights)

so the constraint is: **escheat overrides breakage-take**. the more a dormant prepaid balance reads as
escheatable unclaimed property (paid-for, cash-refundable, owner-identifiable), the less of it a seller may
book as its own revenue. a purchased, dormant balance is precisely the case where the state, not the
seller, has first claim.

```
UNSPENT DORMANT BALANCE
        │
  is it escheatable?
   ┌────┴─────┐
   ▼          ▼
  YES         NO (remote, closed-loop, no owner record)
   │          │
   ▼          ▼
 remit to    breakage → take
 the state   (the seller may recognize as revenue)
 (no take)
```

## contrast — the closed-loop / merchandise-credit treatment

a **closed-loop** credit — redeemable **only** for the seller's own good or service, never convertible to cash —
sits on softer escheat ground than a cash-refundable balance. some states exempt an unredeemed
merchant credit and vest the leftover in the issuer. Florida's unclaimed-property chapter is an example
that does exactly that for a gift certificate / credit memo, but with a **carve-back** that a certain
class **is** still reportable:

> "an unredeemed gift certificate or credit memo as defined in s. 501.95 is not required to be reported
> as unclaimed property."

> "The consideration paid for an unredeemed gift certificate or credit memo is the property of the
> issuer of the unredeemed gift certificate or credit memo."

> "a gift certificate or credit memo described in s. 501.95(2)(b) shall be reported as unclaimed
> property. The consideration paid for such a gift certificate or credit memo is the property of the
> owner of the gift certificate or credit memo."

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 717.1045 — gift certificates and similar credit items](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/717.1045)

so a **closed-loop merchant credit** can vest in the issuer (the take survives) — but a **paid-for,
cash-refundable** balance reads closer to the § 501.95(2)(b) carve-back that **is** reportable. the line
tracks the refund terms: a non-refundable, use-only credit sits where the take survives; a
cash-refundable balance sits where it escheats.

## two representative state regimes — the divergence

state regimes reach a **free point** by their loyalty-exclusion route, but a **paid-for balance** diverges.
Florida and Indiana are two representative examples of how the mechanics differ; neither is a nationwide
rule, and a 50-state program needs a per-state review.

### Florida — gift-cert exemption, with the 5-year dormancy and the carve-back

Florida's general presumption of abandonment runs on a **5-year** dormancy:

> "All intangible property...that is held, issued, or owing in the ordinary course of the holder's
> business and the owner fails to claim such property for more than 5 years after the property becomes
> payable or distributable is presumed unclaimed."

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 717.102 — presumption of abandonment (5-year dormancy)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/717.102)

a **closed-loop** unredeemed credit can vest in the issuer under § 717.1045 (above) — but the § 501.95(2)(b)
carve-back and the paid-for character push a cash-refundable prepaid balance toward
reportable.

### Indiana — a credit balance IS "property"; only the free loyalty card is excluded

Indiana excludes a **loyalty card** and a **gift card** from "property" — but expressly lists a **credit
balance / customer's overpayment / security deposit** as property that CAN escheat. the exclusion is only
for the free, no-consideration instruments:

> "Property means tangible property described in section 8 of this chapter or a fixed and certain
> interest in intangible property held, issued, or owed in the course of a holder's business"

with the exclusion limited to the free instruments:

> "a financial organization loyalty program, a loyalty card, a gift card, or an in-store credit for
> returned merchandise"

and the loyalty card defined by **no consideration** and **no monetization**:

> "Loyalty card means a record given without direct monetary consideration under an award, reward,
> benefit, loyalty, incentive, rebate, or promotional program which may be used or redeemed only to
> obtain goods or services or a discount on goods or services. The term does not include a record that
> may be redeemed for money or otherwise monetized by the issuer."

Indiana's own statute lists the paid-for balances that remain property — "credit balance, customer's
overpayment, security deposit, refund, credit memorandum."

**source**: [Ind. Code § 32-34-1.5-3 — RUUPA definitions (FindLaw)](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-32-property/in-code-sect-32-34-1-5-3/)

a **paid-for prepaid balance** is not a loyalty card and not a gift card — it reads as a credit
balance / customer's overpayment → property that CAN escheat in Indiana. and the dormancy clock is
short: the credit-memo dormancy is **3 years** in Indiana (vs 5 in Florida):

> "Indiana Credit Memos Dormancy Period: Credit Memos: 3"

**source**: [NAUPA credit-memos dormancy periods by state — unclaimed.org](https://unclaimed.org/property-type-credit-memos/)

### the divergence table

| axis | Florida | Indiana |
|------|---------|---------|
| free point (no consideration) | loyalty/gift-cert exclusion (points hazard) | loyalty card excluded from "property" |
| paid-for prepaid balance | closed-loop credit may vest in issuer (§ 717.1045); cash-refundable ↔ § 501.95(2)(b) carve-back reportable | reads as a credit balance / overpayment → escheatable |
| general dormancy | **5 years** (§ 717.102) | credit-memo dormancy **3 years** |
| breakage-take on a dormant balance | survives for a closed-loop credit; capped where reportable | capped — the state reaches a credit balance |

same destination for the **free point** (does not escheat), but the **paid-for balance** diverges — and
in **both** examples, a cash-refundable dormant balance is the escheatable, take-capped profile.

---

## severity

**conditional.** for a **closed-loop, non-refundable** prepaid balance that vests in the issuer under a
state gift-cert exemption, severity is **lower** — the take can survive. for a **cash-refundable**
prepaid balance that goes dormant with an identifiable owner, severity **rises** — it reads as an
escheatable credit balance, the state takes it, and breakage-take is capped. the hazard is a function of
the **refund design** and the **dormancy clock**, not the balance label.

---

## red flags

| red flag | why it raises escheat exposure on the balance |
|----------|----------------------------------------------|
| a customer can get a **cash refund** of the unspent balance | the balance is cash-refundable → not a closed-loop credit → escheatable |
| a dormant balance is **let sit** past the dormancy clock | 3 years (IN credit memo) / 5 years (FL general) triggers the presumption |
| the balance has an **identifiable owner record** | the state can name the owner → reportable unclaimed property |
| a seller **books the dormant balance as its own revenue** before a state escheat check | breakage-take is overridden where the state has first claim |
| the credit is marketed as a **stored / loadable balance** | reads as stored value (paid-for) → outside the loyalty-card exclusion |

---

## the honest gap

- the exact escheat treatment of a prepaid balance is **fact-specific** — whether it is
  cash-refundable, whether an owner is identifiable, and the dormancy trigger all matter, and differ by
  state. confirm with counsel per state before reliance.
- no case law was found that applies RUUPA to a modern prepaid balance with a dual
  closed-loop / refundable design; the statutory text maps cleanly (a paid-for balance is not a loyalty
  card), but it is a text-level read a CPA and counsel should confirm.
- the § 717.1045 issuer carve-out for a closed-loop credit is real, but the § 501.95(2)(b) carve-back and
  Florida legislation queued to narrow expiration carve-outs mean the current text must be confirmed.
- the breakage-take override (escheat first) is stated in the RevenueHub ASC 606 analysis and mirrored in
  10-K practice; a non-retailer issuer's specific dormancy models differ, but the override is
  the transferable point.

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| does a dormant prepaid balance escheat? | it **can** — it is paid-for, so no loyalty-card exclusion; reads as a credit balance / stored value |
| why is a prepaid balance different from a point? | the point is free (no consideration → loyalty exclusion); the balance is **paid for** (direct monetary consideration) |
| what does escheat do to "breakage → take"? | it **caps** it — if the balance escheats, the state takes it, not the seller |
| the closed-loop contrast? | a non-refundable, use-only credit can vest in the issuer (FL § 717.1045); a cash-refundable balance escheats |
| FL mechanic? | 5-year general dormancy (§ 717.102); closed-loop credit may vest in issuer, § 501.95(2)(b) class reportable |
| IN mechanic? | a credit balance / overpayment IS property (§ 32-34-1.5-3); credit-memo dormancy 3 years |
| what keeps the take? | a **closed-loop, non-refundable** credit stays where the take survives; a cash-refundable balance escheats (per-state) |
| severity? | conditional — lower for a closed-loop credit, higher for a dormant cash-refundable deposit |
