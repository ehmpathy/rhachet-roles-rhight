# define.deferred-revenue-on-prepaid-deposits-and-breakage

## .what

when a customer **prepays** a seller for a future good or service, that prepayment is **not revenue yet**.
the seller has been paid for a good or service it has not delivered. under ASC 606 the balance is a
**contract liability** — deferred revenue — recognized as revenue only as the good or service is delivered
(or on expiry / forfeiture). this is the generic law of a prepaid balance: a prepaid gift card, prepaid
airline miles, a prepaid ad account, a prepaid phone balance all sit on the same rule.

this brief is about a **prepaid BALANCE** (paid for in advance), distinct from a reward **POINT** (a
debt a party owes, earned not paid for). three moves anchor a prepaid balance's books:

- **(1) book a contract liability at prepayment** — cash in, deferred revenue up; no revenue recognized
  until the good or service is delivered.
- **(2) where the balance is REFUNDABLE, it carries a REFUND-LIABILITY character** —
  a portion the seller expects to refund is not revenue, it is a payable back to the customer.
- **(3) recognize BREAKAGE (the unspent slice) under ASC 606-10-55-46 to 55-49** — proportional where a
  breakage amount is expected; when-remote where it is not. **but escheat overrides**: where the unspent
  balance must go to the state, breakage income is not available.

## .why

- a prepayment for a not-yet-delivered good or service is the textbook contract liability — a mis-record of
  it as revenue at prepayment overstates income and understates the obligation to stand ready
- a refundable balance changes the character of a slice from deferred revenue to a refund
  liability — a distinct line the state and the customer can both reach
- breakage recognition is when-driven and estimate-driven; escheat can pull the breakage income off the
  table entirely

## (1) the prepaid balance is a contract liability (deferred revenue)

ASC 606 names the balance directly: a prepayment for a future service is a **contract liability**, also
called deferred revenue.

> "A contract liability is an entity's obligation to transfer goods or services to a customer (1) when
> the customer prepays consideration or (2) when the customer's consideration is due for goods and
> services that the entity will yet provide (ASC 606-10-45-2)—whichever happens earlier."

> "A contract liability may be called deferred revenue, unearned revenue, or refund liability."

**source**: [presentation of contract assets and contract liabilities (ASC 606-10-45-2) — RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/presentation-of-contract-assets-and-contract-liabilities)

a CPA-level treatment of customer advances states the same rule — a prepaid amount sits as a liability
until the service is delivered, never revenue at prepayment:

> "debit the cash account (showing the money came in) and credit an unearned revenue account, sometimes
> called a contract liability."

> "Customer advances sit on your balance sheet as liabilities until earned."

> "A business cannot move money from the liability column to the revenue column until it has done what it
> promised."

**source**: [customer advances — accounting and tax rules — LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/what-are-customer-advances-accounting-and-tax-rules/)

and a second accounting treatment ties the deferred-revenue name to the same prepayment trigger:

> "You recognize a contract liability the moment a customer pays you for goods or services you haven't
> delivered yet."

> "As you deliver goods or perform services—in other words, as you satisfy your performance
> obligations—you can start recognizing that cash as revenue."

**source**: [ASC 606 contract liabilities explained — Hubifi](https://www.hubifi.com/blog/asc-606-contract-liabilities-guide)

so a customer's prepayment — a gift-card load, a block of prepaid miles, a funded ad account, a topped-up
phone balance — lands as deferred revenue, recognized as the seller delivers the good or service.

| event | debit | credit | revenue recognized? |
|-------|-------|--------|---------------------|
| customer prepays for a future good/service | cash | deferred revenue (contract liability) | **no** |
| the seller delivers the good/service | deferred revenue | revenue | **yes**, as delivered |
| balance expires / forfeits (no escheat) | deferred revenue | breakage revenue | **yes**, per 55-46 to 55-49 |
| balance expires but escheat applies | deferred revenue | payable to the state | **no** — remit, not revenue |

---

## (2) a refundable prepaid balance carries a refund-liability character

where a prepaid balance is **refundable** (the customer may reclaim the unspent portion — as with a
cash-refundable gift card in a state that requires it, or a returnable ad-account balance), the portion
the seller expects to refund is **not** deferred revenue that converts to income — it is a **refund
liability**, a payable back to the customer. ASC 606 measures it at the consideration the entity does not
expect to be entitled to:

> "A refund liability should be recognized if the entity receives consideration from a customer and
> expects to refund some or all of that consideration to the customer."

> "measured at the amount of consideration received (or receivable) for which the entity does not expect
> to be entitled (that is, amounts not included in the transaction price)."

**source**: [rights of return and customer acceptance in ASC 606 — RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/rights-of-return-and-customer-acceptance)

so a refundable prepaid balance splits: the part the seller will earn is deferred revenue; the part it
expects to refund is a refund liability. the character matters — a refund liability is the customer's money
back, not a slice the seller may recognize as its own revenue.

| slice of a refundable prepaid balance | ASC 606 character | can become seller revenue? |
|---------------------------------------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| the part the seller expects to earn (deliver the good/service) | deferred revenue / contract liability | yes, as delivered |
| the part the seller expects to refund | refund liability | no — payable back to the customer |
| the part unspent, forfeited, not refunded, not escheated | breakage | yes, per 55-46 to 55-49 |
| the part unspent that the state claims | escheat payable | no — remit to the state |

---

## (3) breakage recognition under ASC 606-10-55-46 to 55-49

breakage is the **unspent, forfeited** slice an issuer expects a customer never to use. ASC 606 gives
**two** recognition methods, and which one applies turns on whether the issuer **expects** a breakage amount.

**method A — proportional (breakage amount expected).** where the issuer expects to be entitled to a breakage
amount, it recognizes that breakage in proportion to the pattern of rights the customer exercises:

> "The entity recognizes breakage 'in proportion to the pattern of rights exercised' as customers redeem
> their gift cards."

**method B — when-remote (no breakage amount expected).** where the issuer does **not** expect a breakage
amount, it waits until redemption is unlikely:

> "Breakage revenue is recognized when 'the likelihood of the customer exercising remaining rights
> becomes remote,' typically after extended periods of inactivity defined by state law."

**source**: [gift card breakage accounting — ASC 606 methods — Hubifi](https://www.hubifi.com/blog/gift-card-breakage-accounting-guide)

a revenue-recognition analysis states the same two-method rule and stresses that upfront recognition is
wrong — the entity must stand ready to deliver:

> "The entity should reduce the standalone selling price for estimated breakage. Breakage revenue would
> then be recognized in proportion to the actual performance that the entity provides."

> "The entity should recognize revenue on breakage amounts when the likelihood of the customer
> exercising his or her remaining rights becomes remote."

**source**: [Unexercised Rights (Breakage) in ASC 606 — RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/unexercised-rights)

| breakage method | trigger | when recognized |
|-----------------|---------|-----------------|
| **A — proportional** | the issuer **expects** a breakage amount | in proportion to the pattern of rights exercised (as service is redeemed) |
| **B — when-remote** | the issuer does **not** expect a breakage amount | when the likelihood of the customer exercising remaining rights becomes remote |

## the escheat override — breakage is not available if the state claims it

breakage income is **conditional on the state not reaching it first**. where the unspent balance is
escheatable unclaimed property, the issuer must remit it — a payable, not revenue. the breakage guidance
states the override:

> "If breakage amounts will be owed to the state, then a company should follow the normal process of
> accounting for breakage amounts with one major difference: when the entity would normally recognize
> revenue, a liability for breakage amounts payable to the government should be recognized instead."

**source**: [Unexercised Rights (Breakage) in ASC 606 — RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/article/unexercised-rights)

and a second breakage treatment states the same limit plainly:

> "if a state claims unclaimed gift card balances, your business cannot recognize that amount as breakage
> income."

**source**: [gift card breakage accounting — escheat limit — Hubifi](https://www.hubifi.com/blog/gift-card-breakage-accounting-guide)

whether the state reaches a dormant prepaid balance is an escheat question — and because a **paid-for**
balance does not get the loyalty-card exclusion, the escheat exposure is real. an unclaimed-property
statute may name a paid-for **credit balance** as property that can escheat, and confine the exclusion to
the free, no-consideration instruments. one such example is Indiana's:

> "Loyalty card means a record given without direct monetary consideration under an award, reward,
> benefit, loyalty, incentive, rebate, or promotional program which may be used or redeemed only to
> obtain goods or services or a discount on goods or services. The term does not include a record that
> may be redeemed for money or otherwise monetized by the issuer."

**source**: [Ind. Code § 32-34-1.5-3 — RUUPA definitions (FindLaw)](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-32-property/in-code-sect-32-34-1-5-3/)

a paid-for prepaid balance is paid-for → not a loyalty card → the escheat override can pull the breakage
income off the table. the per-state escheat analysis is a separate question.

## why the material-right frame matters here

a prepaid balance is a **prepayment for a future good or service** — the same material-right mechanic ASC
606 applies to a customer option, where the customer pays in advance and the entity holds a contract
liability until it performs:

> "If the option provides a material right to the customer, the customer in effect pays the entity in
> advance for future goods or services"

> "a portion of the revenue from the transaction should be allocated to the loyalty points and recognized
> when the points are redeemed or expire."

**source**: [PwC Viewpoint — customer options / material right (ASC 606-10-55-42)](https://viewpoint.pwc.com/dt/us/en/pwc/accounting_guides/revenue_from_contrac/revenue_from_contrac_US/chapter_7_options_to_US/72customer_options_t_US.html)

the balance's revenue is recognized **as the good or service is delivered (or on expiry)** — the same
"prepay-then-perform" shape, applied to a paid-for balance rather than an earned point.

## how an earned point and a paid-for balance differ

| axis | reward POINT (earned) | prepaid BALANCE (paid-for) |
|------|--------------------------------|------------------------------|
| how the balance arises | **earned** for free (no consideration) | **paid for** (direct monetary consideration) |
| balance-sheet shape | accrued liability or deferred revenue (own obligation) | contract liability (deferred revenue), refund-liability slice if refundable |
| when revenue recognized | at **redemption or expiry** of the point | as the **good or service is delivered**, or on expiry / forfeiture |
| breakage | modeled on unredeemed points | modeled on the unspent balance, per 55-46 to 55-49 |
| escheat exposure | free point fits the loyalty-card exclusion → low | paid-for balance gets **no** exclusion → the override bites harder |

same ASC 606 machinery, different origin: the point is earned, the balance is purchased. the purchased
character is what makes the escheat override a live constraint on a prepaid balance's breakage.

## the honest gap

- the (deferred-revenue vs refund-liability) split is fact-driven and a CPA should confirm it — it turns
  on the exact refund terms in the prepayment contract. a fully non-refundable balance is
  cleaner deferred revenue; a refundable one carries the refund-liability slice.
- the breakage method (proportional vs when-remote) turns on whether a seller can **estimate** an expected
  breakage amount with enough history; a young program may lack the data for method A and default to B.
- the escheat override is stated in the RevenueHub and Hubifi analyses and mirrored in 10-K practice, but
  whether a specific dormant balance escheats is a per-state legal question.
- this is a text-level read of ASC 606 and the unclaimed-property statutes, strong but CPA- and
  counsel-confirmable; no guidance addresses any specific prepaid-balance product.

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| how is a prepaid balance booked? | as a **contract liability** (deferred revenue) — not revenue at prepayment |
| when is the revenue recognized? | as the **good or service is delivered**, or on expiry / forfeiture |
| what if the balance is refundable? | the expected-refund slice is a **refund liability**, not deferred revenue |
| how is breakage recognized? | **proportional** if a breakage amount is expected; **when-remote** if not (55-46 to 55-49) |
| what pulls breakage income off the table? | **escheat** — if the state claims the balance, remit it; do not recognize revenue |
| how does a prepaid balance differ from a point? | the balance is **paid for** (no loyalty-card exclusion); the point is **earned** (exclusion fits) |
