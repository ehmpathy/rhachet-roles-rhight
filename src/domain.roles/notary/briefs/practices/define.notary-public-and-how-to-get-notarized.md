# define.notary-public-and-how-to-get-notarized

## .what

a notary public is a state-commissioned public officer who acts as an impartial witness to the execution of documents.

the worked example: a founder in **Texas** who must submit a **notarized affidavit** to the **Delaware** Division of Corporations to correct a franchise tax assessment on a void C-corp. the two-state split forces the interstate-acceptance question.

⚠️ **the Texas figures are Texas figures.** fee caps, RON authorization, and the location rule are all state law. if you are not in Texas, treat the Texas sections as a worked template and re-verify against your own Secretary of State.

### RECOGNITION vs SUFFICIENCY

**29 Del. C. §4324** answers whether Delaware **recognizes the ACT** — the easy
half. **§4328(c)** governs whether the **CERTIFICATE** is good enough, and it is a
**conjunction**: (a) **and** (b) **and** any one of (c)(1)–(4) (quoted in full in
`../jurats/inventory.of=jurat.jurisdiction=usa-delaware.md`). so the foreign
**form** is accepted — and every §4328(a) element still binds. one of them is the
trap:

> **§4328(a)(5)** — "If the notarial officer is a notary public, indicate the
> **date of expiration**, if any, of the officer's commission."

⚠️ **Florida's statutory jurat form has no such field** — the expiry lives on the
**seal**. so a recognized act can still arrive on a certificate a Delaware clerk
may refuse. **recognition is rarely the problem; an absent ELEMENT is.** the
check is in the ronproviders brief.

## .what a notary public is

Texas defines the office directly.

> "a public officer of the state commissioned by the Office of the Texas Secretary of State that administers oaths, takes acknowledgments, witness(es) signatures, and performs other duties as permitted by law, for paper (tangible) documents."
>
> — [Texas Secretary of State: Notary Public](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/notary-public.shtml)

the National Notary Association frames the same office as

> "an official appointed by a state government to serve the public as an impartial witness"
>
> — [NNA: What is a Notary Public](https://www.nationalnotary.org/knowledge-center/about-notaries/what-is-a-notary-public)

| fact | detail | source |
|------|--------|--------|
| who commissions | the state — in Texas, the Secretary of State | [TX SoS](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/notary-public.shtml) |
| is a notary a lawyer? | no. a commission is not a law license | [NNA](https://www.nationalnotary.org/knowledge-center/about-notaries/what-is-a-notary-public) |
| what a commission is | a term-limited state appointment to perform notarial acts | [TX SoS](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/notary-public.shtml) |
| can a notary give legal advice? | no — a notary may not give legal advice or draft legal papers | [NYC DCA: Notario Publico](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/consumers/Consumers-Notario-Publico.pdf) |

the "notario publico" trap: in civil-law countries a *notario* is a trained lawyer; in the US a notary is **not**.

## .the historical origin

| era | form | function |
|-----|------|----------|
| Roman Republic | *scribae* | scribes who recorded facts and judicial proceedings, transcribed state papers, registered decrees and judgments of magistrates |
| ~100 AD | *notarius* | one who took statements in Latin shorthand (*notae tironianae*, named for Cicero's secretary Tiro) and wrote them out as memoranda |
| post-5th century | continental notary | survived the collapse of the Western Empire as a figure of importance in Europe |
| 12th century onward | civil-law notary | when civil law revived in medieval Italy, the notary became a central institution — a position it still holds in civil-law countries |

> "The office has its origin in the civil institutions of ancient Rome."
>
> — [Hugill & IP: The Notary Public — A 5,000 Year Old Story](https://www.hugillandip.com/2020/07/the-notary-public-a-5000-year-old-story/)

sources: [Hugill & IP](https://www.hugillandip.com/2020/07/the-notary-public-a-5000-year-old-story/), [Notary NI: History of Notaries](https://notaryni.com/history-of-notaries-explained/), [Wikipedia: Scriba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriba_(ancient_Rome))

### the modern function

| function | what it buys |
|----------|--------------|
| fraud deterrence | a stranger with a stamp and a journal raises the cost of forgery |
| identity verification | the notary inspects a government photo ID |
| free-will and competence check | the notary confirms the person is not coerced and appears competent |
| self-authentication | the document can enter evidence without a live witness to prove the signature |

under the Federal Rules of Evidence, certain items need "no extrinsic evidence of authenticity in order to be admitted" — and item (8) covers

> "A document accompanied by a certificate of acknowledgment that is lawfully executed by a notary public or another officer who is authorized to take acknowledgments."
>
> — [Cornell LII: FRE 902](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_902)

the economic point of the office: a court need not summon the witness who watched you sign.

## .what notarization proves — and what it does not

| notarization proves | notarization does NOT prove |
|---------------------|-----------------------------|
| a specific identified person appeared before the notary | that the contents of the document are true |
| that person was identified by ID or personal knowledge | that the document is legal, valid, or enforceable |
| that person signed, or acknowledged a prior signature | that the notary read or approved the document |
| for a jurat: that person swore or affirmed the contents | that a state has "approved" the document |

the canonical formulation — Cal. Civil Code § 1189 requires this notice in a box at the top of every acknowledgment certificate:

> "A notary public or other officer completing this certificate verifies only the identity of the individual who signed the document to which this certificate is attached, and not the truthfulness, accuracy, or validity of that document."
>
> — [Cal. Civ. Code § 1189](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1189)

so: **the notary vouches for the person, not the paper.**

the liability for a false affidavit does not vanish — it lands on the affiant, via perjury exposure created by the oath. the notary just proves the oath happened.

## .the two common notarial acts

| | acknowledgment | jurat |
|---|---|---|
| what the person declares | "i signed this, of my own free will, and i know what it is" | "i swear/affirm the contents are true" |
| oath or affirmation? | no | **yes** — the notary must administer it |
| must the signature happen in front of the notary? | **no** — may be pre-signed and later acknowledged | **yes** — must be signed in the notary's presence |
| typical use | deeds, powers of attorney, contracts, corporate consents | affidavits, sworn statements, declarations |
| perjury exposure | no | yes |

> "A jurat is a notarial act in which a person declares to a notary public that the contents of a written document signed by the person are truthful."
>
> — [Superior Notary: Jurats and Acknowledgements](https://www.superiornotaryservices.com/blog/jurats-and-acknowledgements/)

other, rarer acts: copy certification, proof by witness, protest of negotiable instruments.

### an affidavit needs a jurat

an affidavit is by definition a sworn statement. therefore:

1. do **not** sign the affidavit in advance
2. appear before the notary
3. the notary administers the oath or affirmation out loud — you answer
4. you sign, in the notary's view
5. the notary completes the jurat certificate, applies the seal, and dates it

⚠️ if you pre-sign an affidavit, a careful notary will make you sign again or refuse the jurat. a sloppy notary will stamp it anyway — and the defect can be raised later. do not pre-sign.

## .how to get a document notarized, in person

| step | detail |
|------|--------|
| 1. print the document | complete, with all blanks filled, but **unsigned** |
| 2. confirm the certificate | make sure a jurat block is on it (or that the notary will attach a loose certificate) |
| 3. take a valid photo ID | unexpired, government-issued: driver license, passport, state ID |
| 4. take the fee | small cash amount; some venues are free |
| 5. appear in person | answer the oath, then sign in front of the notary |
| 6. keep the original | the seal on the original is what matters — mail the original if the agency wants it |

⚠️ if more than one person must swear, each must appear with their own ID.

### where to find one

| venue | typical cost | notes |
|-------|--------------|-------|
| your bank or credit union | often **free for account holders** | the cheapest reliable option; call ahead — not every branch staffs one |
| UPS Store | paid, per signature | wide coverage; appointment scheduled online at many stores |
| AAA branch | free for members (limits apply) | membership required |
| public library | often free | availability varies widely by branch |
| county clerk / city offices | free or nominal | best when the document goes to a court or public record |
| parcel and print shops | paid | ad-hoc availability |
| mobile notary | travel fee on top of the act fee | they come to you; good for the immobile or the time-poor |

sources: [LegalClarity: where to get a document notarized](https://legalclarity.org/where-to-get-a-document-notarized-banks-libraries-more/), [Crediful: banks that notarize for free](https://www.crediful.com/banks-that-notarize-for-free/), [AAA notary services](https://mwg.aaa.com/more-services/notary-services), [The UPS Store: notary](https://www.theupsstore.com/print/notary)

⚠️ inferred, unconfirmed: the "free for members" claims at banks and AAA are reported by secondary sources and vary by branch and account tier. verify with the specific branch by phone.

## .cost

notary fees are capped by statute in most states. Texas caps them in Gov't Code § 406.024.

| act | Texas statutory fee |
|-----|---------------------|
| acknowledgment or proof, with certificate and seal | $10 first signature, $1 each additional signature |
| oath or affirmation with certificate and seal (**the jurat you need**) | $10 |
| certificate under seal, not otherwise provided for | $10 |
| protest of a bill or note | $4 |
| copies of records | $1 per page |

— [Tex. Gov't Code § 406.024](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_406.024)

⚠️ subsection (c) directs the Secretary of State to adjust these amounts every five years for inflation, so the effective cap may exceed the base numbers above. the current adjusted schedule was not located.

| line item | typical |
|-----------|---------|
| in-person at a bank, as a customer | $0 |
| in-person at a retail venue in Texas | ~$10 per signature (statutory cap) |
| mobile notary travel fee | not capped by § 406.024 — negotiated separately |
| online (Texas online notary) | up to **$25** for the online notarization, **in addition to** the regular notary fee |

> "Online notaries may charge up to $25 for each notarization they perform in addition to regular notary charges."
>
> — [Texas SoS: Online Notary Public Educational Information](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/online-np-educational.shtml)

the statute behind that cap is Tex. Gov't Code § 406.111.

the TX SoS page gives the worked example: $25 + $10 = **$35** for one signature online.

## .remote online notarization (RON)

RON is a notarial act performed over a live two-way audio-video link instead of physical co-presence.

| component | what happens |
|-----------|--------------|
| live audio-video link | notary and signer see and hear each other in real time |
| credential analysis | the ID is photographed and machine-checked for authenticity features |
| identity proofing / KBA | knowledge-based authentication — out-of-wallet questions drawn from public/credit data |
| electronic signature | signer applies a signature to the electronic document in-session |
| digital seal + certificate | notary applies an electronic seal backed by an X.509 PKI digital certificate |
| session record | the audio-video session is recorded and retained by the notary |

Texas mandates each of these. per Tex. Gov't Code § 406.110 the online notary must verify identity via two-way video and audio conference technology, and — absent personal knowledge — must obtain **all three** of remote presentation of a government ID, credential analysis of that ID, and identity proofing of the person. see [§ 406.110](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_406.110) and [§ 406.108](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_406.108) (record retention).

### does Texas permit it? — **yes, confirmed**

| fact | detail | source |
|------|--------|--------|
| Texas has an "online notary public" commission | a separate authorization layered on a traditional commission | [TX SoS: Notary Public](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/notary-public.shtml) |
| prerequisite | "You must already hold a current commission as a notary public in the State of Texas." | [TX SoS: Start as an Online Notary](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/gettingstarted.shtml) |
| application fee (paid by the notary, not you) | $50 plus 2.7% convenience fee | [TX SoS: Start as an Online Notary](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/gettingstarted.shtml) |
| where the **notary** must be | "In order for a Texas notary public to perform an online notarization, the notary must be physically located within the boundaries of Texas at the time of notarization." | [TX SoS: Online NP Educational](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/online-np-educational.shtml) |
| where the **signer** may be | anywhere — not restricted to Texas | [TX SoS: Online NP Educational](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/statdoc/online-np-educational.shtml) |
| record retention | at least five years, incl. the audio-video session record | [Tex. Gov't Code § 406.108](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._gov't_code_section_406.108) |

that location rule matters a lot for the interstate analysis below: a Texas RON act is legally **performed in Texas**, even though the signer may be elsewhere.

### how many states permit RON?

⚠️ **could not confirm a single authoritative count.** sources disagree materially:

| source | claim | date |
|--------|-------|------|
| [Proof (vendor)](https://www.proof.com/blog/is-remote-online-notarization-legal-in-all-states) | 42 states + DC with effective RON laws; AL, CT, GA, MS, SC lack effective laws | "as of late 2025" |
| [NotaryCam (vendor)](https://www.notarycam.com/what-states-allow-remote-notary/) | 47 states + DC | 2026 |
| [NotaryPublic24 (secondary)](https://www.notarypublic24.com/remote-online-notarization-ron-us-2026/) | 49 states + DC permanent; California the lone holdout until 2030 | 2026 |

all three are commercial or secondary. the safe statement: **the large majority of states authorize RON, Texas and Delaware both do, and the exact national count is contested.**

⛔ **California does not, yet** — confirmed against the code:

> **Cal. Gov't Code §8231(b)** — an online notarization platform "shall not be
> authorized for use … **prior to registration with the Secretary of State or
> prior to completion of the Secretary of State's technology project** … The
> Secretary of State shall complete the technology project **no later than
> January 1, 2030**"

the agency agrees: *"The new law does not take effect until the Secretary of
State certifies that it has completed the technology project … or on January 1,
2030 (whichever comes first)."* so a California signer who follows the Texas
template books a RON that **cannot lawfully be performed**. cite §8231(b), never
the vendor page — see `../jurats/inventory.of=jurat.jurisdiction=usa-california.md`.

when you must lean on a secondary source, verify its most **specific** claim — a
named state, mechanism, and date is one pull to check; a bare count is not.

## .the interstate question — highest-stakes uncertainty

the question: will the **Delaware** Division of Corporations accept an affidavit notarized remotely by a **Texas** online notary?

### what i confirmed

**1. Delaware recognizes out-of-state notarial acts by statute.**

> "A notarial act performed in another state has the same effect under the law of this State as if performed by a notarial officer of this State"
>
> — [29 Del. C. § 4324](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title29/c043/sc02/index.shtml)

§4324 then grades the proof by officer type: (b) makes the signature and title
**prima facie** for any officer; (c) makes the authority **conclusive** ⛔ only for
a notary public (a)(1) or a judge/clerk (a)(2) — never for an (a)(3) officer. the
quotes and the grade table: `../jurats/inventory.of=jurat.jurisdiction=usa-delaware.md`.
(c) settles the **officer's authority**, not whether the **certificate** carries
the §4328(a) elements — see the read-this-first section above.

**2. Delaware itself authorizes RON.** 29 Del. C. § 4320 permits a remotely located individual to appear before a Delaware notarial officer by communication technology, subject to identity verification and audio-visual record requirements. — [29 Del. C. subch. II](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title29/c043/sc02/index.shtml)

**3. the Delaware Division of Corporations rejects remote/electronic notarization — but only for apostille.**

> "Please be advised we will not accept documents that are notarized remotely or electronically for apostille."
>
> — [Delaware Division of Corporations: Certifications, Apostilles and Authentication](https://corp.delaware.gov/apost_info/)

the same page lists "Out-of-state documents, this includes notarized by out-of-state notaries" among documents that cannot be apostilled or authenticated there. **the scope of both statements on that page is the apostille/authentication service**, not general correspondence or franchise-tax submissions.

**4. the federal fix is not law.** the SECURE Notarization Act of 2025 would require states to recognize other states' notarizations that affect interstate commerce, and require federal courts to recognize notarizations by any state's notarial officers. it has **not** been enacted — [H.R. 1777](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1777) and [S. 1561](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1561/text) sit in committee in the 119th Congress. do not rely on it.

**5. RULONA is a model act, adopted unevenly.** the Uniform Law Commission's Revised Uniform Law on Notarial Acts supplies the "act performed in another state" recognition language that Delaware's § 4324 tracks. ⚠️ i could not confirm the current enactment count from the ULC directly; secondary reports put some version of ULONA/RULONA at roughly 28 states as of early 2025 — treat that number as unverified.

### what i inferred

⚠️ **a construction, not case law:** a Texas RON act is a "notarial act performed in another state" for purposes of 29 Del. C. § 4324, because Texas law places the notary physically in Texas at the moment of the act. Delaware's own RON statute uses the same frame:

> **§4320(c)** — "A notarial officer **located in this State** may use
> communication technology to perform a notarial act for a remotely located
> individual if all of the following apply…"

so Delaware locates its own remote acts by where the **officer** is. no Delaware case, opinion, or administrative statement that confirms the chain was found.

⚠️ **inferred, unconfirmed:** the apostille-specific rejection on corp.delaware.gov does **not** govern franchise-tax correspondence. i found no statement that extends it to franchise-tax or reinstatement submissions — but i also found no statement that limits it. absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

⚠️ **inferred, unconfirmed:** a clerk may apply the department's apostille habit reflexively, regardless of what § 4324 says. the clerk question, not the legal one, decides whether your paper is processed this month.

### summary

| question | status |
|----------|--------|
| is a Texas RON valid in Texas? | **confirmed yes** |
| does Delaware statute recognize out-of-state notarial acts? | **confirmed yes**, § 4324 |
| is a foreign **notary public's** authority conclusively established? | **confirmed yes** — §4324(c), and ⛔ only for (a)(1)/(a)(2) |
| does § 4324 reach out-of-state *remote* acts? | ⚠️ construction, not a holding — §4320(c)'s "located in this State" frame supports it |
| ⛔ is the **CERTIFICATE** sufficient once the act is recognized? | **a separate question** — §4328(c) is a conjunction; every §4328(a) element still binds |
| ⛔ does a Florida jurat carry §4328(a)(5), the commission expiry? | **no — not on the certificate.** it is on the **seal**. check the returned PDF |
| does the Division of Corporations reject remote notarization? | **confirmed** — for apostille only |
| does that rejection extend to franchise-tax filings? | ⚠️ **unknown. not confirmed either way.** |
| is there a federal statute that compels acceptance? | **confirmed no** — SECURE Act not enacted |

| your situation | choose | why |
|----------------|--------|-----|
| document goes to an out-of-state government agency, and you have not asked whether remote is accepted | **in-person, wet ink** | zero acceptance risk; ~$0–$10 |

## ⚠️ the wet-ink default does NOT cure the real risk

§4324(c) conclusively establishes a foreign notary public's authority, and
§4320(c) locates an act by where the **officer** is — so the recognition worry
behind a wet-ink default is small. the risk that remains is not cured by wet ink:

| worry | does wet ink fix it? |
|---|---|
| Delaware refuses a **remote** act | ✅ yes — this is what the default was for |
| a clerk applies the apostille habit reflexively | 🚩 partly, and only if the notary is **in Delaware** |
| ⛔ **the certificate lacks §4328(a)(5), the commission expiry** | ⛔ **NO** |

**Florida's statutory jurat form has no expiry field in ink or electronically**,
so a wet-ink Florida notarization carries the identical §4328(a) gap as a
Florida RON.

### what actually decides it

| the real question | the answer |
|---|---|
| does the officer's **seal** render the commission expiration date? | check the artifact — see the ronproviders brief |
| will **this clerk** take a remote act? | the 30-second call below. still the cheapest move on the page |

so the decision table above is a general default for a reader with no statute in
hand. for a Florida → Delaware matter, the ronproviders brief governs.

## .recommendation for the Delaware franchise-tax affidavit

either path (wet ink or RON) works once the seal carries the expiry — confirm it
on the artifact. the wet-ink steps:

1. print the affidavit **unsigned**
2. take an unexpired photo ID
3. go to your bank branch (free for customers) or a UPS Store (~$10)
4. state that you need a **jurat** — an oath, not just an acknowledgment
5. swear, then sign in front of the notary
6. mail the **original** with the seal to the Division of Corporations; keep a scan

### the 30-second call that eliminates the uncertainty

ask the clerk who receives the document:

> "do you accept documents notarized remotely by an out-of-state online notary?"

call the **specific unit** that will receive the paper, not the agency's main
line — for a Delaware franchise-tax matter that is the Division of Corporations
franchise tax group, published at [corp.delaware.gov](https://corp.delaware.gov/).
if a named examiner is already on the file, ask that person.

if they say yes, use RON and save the trip. if they say no or hedge, go
wet-ink. either answer is better than an inference.

⚠️ also worth the same call: confirm whether the Division wants the **original** with a raised/inked seal or accepts a scan by email. that determines whether you mail or attach.
