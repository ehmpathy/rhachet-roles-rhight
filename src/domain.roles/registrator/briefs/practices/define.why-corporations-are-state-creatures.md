# define.why-corporations-are-state-creatures

## .what

corporations in the US are creatures of state law. **there is no general
federal incorporation regime** — Congress never built one.

⚠️ that is a fact about **what exists**, not **what is possible**. Congress *has*
the constitutional power to charter corporations; it has never exercised it
generally. the intuitive explanation — that Congress lacks the power — is the one
the Supreme Court rejected in 1819.

---

## .the core principle

| level | role |
|-------|------|
| **state** | creates the corporation, in practice |
| **federal** | taxes and regulates the corporation |

> "The federal government does not charter corporations (**except National
> Banks, Federal Savings Banks, and Federal Credit Unions**) although it does
> regulate them."
>
> — [Wikipedia: United States Corporate Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_corporate_law) ⚠️ **secondary, background only**

the parenthetical's three standing federal charters already show the power is real — see `.federal exceptions`.

---

## ⛔ .constitutional basis — the intuitive reason is WRONG

the natural explanation: the Constitution never enumerates incorporation, so under the 10th Amendment the power is reserved to the states. **the Supreme Court rejected that by name.**

### the syllabus states the holding in six words

> "Congress has power to incorporate a bank"
>
> — *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), syllabus

### and the opinion addresses the 10th Amendment argument directly

> "Among the enumerated powers, we do not find that of establishing a bank or
> creating a corporation. But there is no phrase in the instrument which, like
> the Articles of Confederation, excludes incidental or implied powers and
> which requires that everything granted shall be expressly and minutely
> described. **Even the 10th Amendment** … **omits the word "expressly,"** and
> declares only that the powers "not delegated to the United States, nor
> prohibited to the States, are reserved to the States or to the people," thus
> leaving the question whether the particular power which may become the
> subject of contest has been delegated to the one Government, or prohibited to
> the other, to depend on a **fair construction of the whole instrument**."
>
> — *McCulloch*, 17 U.S. at 406-407

the 10th Amendment does **not** say "powers not **expressly** delegated." Marshall reads that omission as deliberate — it leaves room for implied powers. so a silence in the enumeration settles no question.

### the sovereignty version fails too

> "There is nothing in the Constitution of the United States similar to the
> Articles of Confederation, which exclude incidental or implied powers."
>
> — *McCulloch*, syllabus

### ⭐ so what IS the real reason?

not constitutional incapacity — **history and politics.**

| the reason | status |
|---|---|
| Congress *cannot* charter corporations | ⛔ **false** — *McCulloch* |
| Congress *has not* created a general federal incorporation regime | ✅ true |
| the states got there first, and no federal statute displaced them | ✅ true |

- the states began general incorporation in 1811 and ran a competitive system long before Congress considered a general one
- the 1908 Hepburn Bill proposed federal incorporation and failed. no constitutional provision required that outcome
- the three live federal charters under `.federal exceptions` prove the power exists; the oldest, national banks, is the charter *McCulloch* upheld

## .historical context

### early america: legislative charters

before the 1800s, each corporation required a special act of the state legislature, chartered for a specific purpose (build a bridge, operate a bank).

> "Prior to the late 19th century, most companies were incorporated by a special bill adopted by legislature."
>
> — [Wikipedia: History of Corporate Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_corporate_law_in_the_United_States)

### 1811: general incorporation begins

New York became the first state to allow general incorporation, **for
manufacturing businesses**, by public registration rather than legislative
approval. general incorporation arrived sector by sector over decades, not all at once.

> "New York was the first state to have a simple public registration procedure to start corporations (not specific permission from the legislature) **for manufacturing business**."
>
> — [Wikipedia: United States Corporate Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_corporate_law)

⚠️ Wikipedia only. the 1811 New York Act is a real statute and has not been pulled — treat the date as a lead.

### 1899: Delaware enters the race

Delaware adopted a permissive general corporation law, which started its rise as the dominant state for incorporation. ⚠️ **uncited.**

### 1908: federal incorporation rejected

the Hepburn Bill of 1908 proposed federal incorporation. it failed; the state-based system remained.

⚠️ **uncited** — and it is the load-bearing fact of this brief (the *actual* reason, per `.constitutional basis`). it should carry a primary source.

## .the internal affairs doctrine

one state's law governs a corporation's internal affairs, even when it operates elsewhere.

| concept | what it means |
|---------|---------------|
| state of incorporation | which state's law governs |
| internal affairs | relationships among shareholders, directors, officers |
| external matters | contracts, torts, employment — governed by where they occur |

> "The internal affairs doctrine is a conflict of laws principle which
> recognizes that only one State should have the authority to regulate a
> corporation's internal affairs — **matters peculiar to the relationships
> among or between the corporation and its current officers, directors, and
> shareholders — because otherwise a corporation could be faced with
> conflicting demands.**"
>
> — *Edgar v. MITE Corp.*, 457 U.S. 624, 645 (1982) (White, J.), **Part V-B**

- quote the Court, not a secondary paraphrase; the clause after *"internal affairs"* carries the **reason** — conflicting demands — and an ellipsis there drops it

### ⚠️ *MITE* is a SPLIT opinion — the precedential weight had to be established

| step | what the opinion says |
|---|---|
| the lineup | *"JUSTICE WHITE delivered an opinion, **Parts I, II, and V-B** of which are the opinion of the Court"* |
| the passage | sits in Part **V**, subsection **B** |
| the count | 5 votes for V-B |

so the quote **is** the opinion of the Court. in Part V-A it would be a plurality view with no precedential force, in identical words — a quote's authority is a property of **where it sits** in the document.

### ⚠️ and note what the Court actually DID with the doctrine

it **declined to apply it**:

> "That doctrine is of little use to the State in this context. Tender offers
> contemplate transfers of stock by stockholders to a third party, and do not
> themselves implicate the internal affairs of the target company."

cite *MITE* for **what the doctrine is**, never for a holding that it controlled an outcome.

this is why a Delaware corporation remains governed by Delaware law regardless of where it operates.

## .what this means for founders

### 1. your corporation is a creature of one state

incorporated in Delaware, your corporation exists because Delaware says it does; governance, shareholder rights, and director duties are Delaware law.

### 2. change states = new entity

you cannot "move" a corporation to another state. you can:
- **domesticate** (some states allow conversion) — ⚠️ **keeps the EIN** if the
  business structure is unchanged; see §3
- **merge** into a new entity in another state
- **dissolve and reform** — a new charter is issued, so a new EIN is required

none of these preserve the original entity unchanged **at the state level** — a statement about the *charter*, which does not decide the federal EIN question. see §3.

### 3. EIN is federal, entity is state

| identifier | issued by | tied to |
|------------|-----------|---------|
| EIN | IRS (federal) | the legal entity |
| entity number | state | state registration |

### ⛔ "new entity = new EIN" is TOO STRONG — the IRS test is narrower

the IRS asks no question about entity identity. it publishes a **list**, and a state-level conversion is on the *keep-your-EIN* side:

> "**Do not** get a new EIN if you: … Reorganize to change only your identity or
> location. **Convert at the state level and don't change your business
> structure.**"
>
> — [IRS, *When to get a new EIN* → Corporations](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/do-you-need-a-new-ein)

⚠️ that page is a **collapsed accordion** — 911 characters plain, 3,611 after `Expand all` (see `.provenance`).

### the printable artifact says it better — and says MORE

> "You will not need a new EIN if any of the following are true: … After a
> corporate reorganization, you only change identity, **form**, or place of
> organization"
>
> — [IRS Pub. 1635, *Understanding Your EIN*](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1635.pdf), p. 6

"form" is in the PDF and absent from the web page. **cite the PDF.**

### what DOES trigger a new EIN

> "You will need a new EIN if any of the following are true: … The corporation
> becomes a partnership or a sole proprietorship · You create a new corporation
> after a statutory merger · **You receive a new corporate charter**"
>
> — Pub. 1635, p. 6

the trigger for dissolve-and-reform is **a new charter** — a concrete, checkable fact.

| what you do | new EIN? | why |
|---|---|---|
| reinstate a void Delaware corp | ⛔ **no** | the charter is revived, not re-issued (8 Del. C. §312(e)) |
| **domesticate / convert at the state level**, structure unchanged | ⛔ **no** | named on the keep-your-EIN list |
| reorganize, changing only identity, form, or place | ⛔ **no** | Pub. 1635, p. 6 |
| dissolve in DE, incorporate fresh in WY | ✅ **yes** | *"you receive a new corporate charter"* |
| the corporation becomes an LLC taxed as a partnership | ✅ **yes** | structure changed |

⚠️ do **not** reason from "is it the same entity?" — reason from the list.

### 4. state selection is legal

states compete, so you can choose which state's laws you want — by design.

## .federal exceptions

the federal government does charter a few specific entity types:

| entity type | federal authority |
|-------------|-------------------|
| national banks | Office of Comptroller of Currency |
| federal credit unions | National Credit Union Administration |
| federal savings banks | OCC |

these are narrow exceptions; general business corporations remain state creatures. they are also the proof, against `.constitutional basis`'s rejected premise, that Congress holds the power.

---

## .why this matters for reinstatement vs reregistration

| action | entity | EIN | the IRS reason |
|--------|--------|-----|----------------|
| reinstate void Delaware corp | same entity | **same EIN** | the charter is revived, never re-issued — 8 Del. C. §312(e) |
| dissolve Delaware, form in WY | new entity | **new EIN** | *"you receive a new corporate charter"* — Pub. 1635 p. 6 |
| domesticate to another state, structure unchanged | varies by state | **same EIN** | *"convert at the state level and don't change your business structure"* |

kill the charter in one state and take out a new one elsewhere: a new entity **and** a new EIN. convert without a structure change: the EIN follows you.

---

## .the race to the bottom (or top?)

states compete for incorporation fees. critics call it a "race to the bottom" — states weaken shareholder protections to attract corporations. defenders call it a "race to the top" — states develop sophisticated corporate law to attract sophisticated businesses. Delaware's Court of Chancery is the prime example.

---

## .summary

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| who creates corporations? | states |
| why not federal? | ⛔ **not** for want of the power — *McCulloch* holds Congress has it. Congress simply never built a general regime, and the states were already there |
| what governs internal affairs? | state of incorporation — *Edgar v. MITE*, Part V-B |
| can you move a corp to another state? | not simply — new entity or conversion |
| why does EIN change when you reregister? | because *"you receive a new corporate charter"* — ⚠️ **not** because "new entity = new tax ID". a state-level conversion keeps the EIN |
