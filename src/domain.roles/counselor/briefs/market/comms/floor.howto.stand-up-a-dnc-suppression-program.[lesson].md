## ⭐ .why — consent does not reach this track, and that is the whole point

the single most consequential misread in this subject: a company with flawless per-number written
consent believes it is covered. **it is not.** `§ 227(c)` is a different statute answering a
different question.

| | `§ 227(b)` — the autodialer track | `§ 227(c)` — this track |
|---|---|---|
| the question | did they **agree** to be contacted? | did they **object**, and did you honor it? |
| the defense | a consent record | ⭐ a **suppression program** |
| does consent answer it? | yes | ⛔ **no — the statutory defense has no consent element at all** |

the subsection's own purpose statement frames it around objection rather than agreement — the
protection of *"residential telephone subscribers' privacy rights to avoid receiving telephone
solicitations to which they object"*.

⭐ **and it is dialer-agnostic.** a manually-dialed live call that sits entirely outside `§ 227(b)`
can still violate this track. a program built only against the autodialer regime has no answer here.

## ⭐ .the payoff — the statute names your program as an affirmative defense

this is what separates this layer from every other one in the subject. everywhere else, the work
reduces your **chance** of liability. here, `§ 227(c)(5)` makes the work itself a **pleaded defense**:

> "It shall be an affirmative defense in any action brought under this paragraph that the defendant
> has established and implemented, **with due care**, reasonable practices and procedures to
> effectively prevent telephone solicitations in violation of the regulations prescribed under this
> subsection."

⚠️ **read the conjunction: "established *and* implemented, with due care."** a written policy nobody
runs does not match the text. the artifact and its exercise are both elements.

### the threshold — one call is not enough

unlike `§ 227(b)`, this right has an entry requirement. `§ 227(c)(5)` reaches:

> "A person who has received **more than one telephone call within any 12-month period** by or on
> behalf of the same entity in violation of the regulations prescribed under this subsection"

⭐ **so the two tracks are not symmetric**, and that difference is material to exposure math: a single
violative call creates no `§ 227(c)` claim, while a single autodialed message can create a `§ 227(b)`
one. damages here are *"up to $500"* per violation, trebled at the court's discretion on a willful or
knowing finding.

## the program — `§ 64.1200(d)`'s six minimum standards

the regulation bars the calls outright unless the caller *"has instituted procedures for maintaining
a list of persons who request not to receive such calls."* those procedures **must meet the following
minimum standards** — so this is a checklist the regulation wrote, not a design of ours.

| # | the standard | what the text requires |
|---|--------------|------------------------|
| 1 | **written policy** | *"must have a written policy, **available upon demand**, for maintaining a do-not-call list"* |
| 2 | **training of personnel** | personnel *"must be informed and trained in the existence and use of the do-not-call list"* |
| 3 | **recording, disclosure** | record the request and list the number *"at the time the request is made"*; honor within *"ten (10) business days"* |
| 4 | **identification of callers** | give *"the name of the individual caller"*, the entity's name, and a contact number or address — and it *"may not be a 900 number"* |
| 5 | ⭐ **affiliated persons** | a request applies to *"the particular entity making the call"* and **not** to affiliates, with one exception (below) |
| 6 | **maintenance** | *"A do-not-call request must be honored for **5 years** from the time the request is made."* |

### ⭐ three provisions inside those standards that carry more weight than their titles suggest

**⛔ you may outsource the list; you may not outsource the liability.** inside standard 3:

> "If such requests are recorded or maintained by a party other than the person or entity on whose
> behalf the call is made, the person or entity on whose behalf the call is made **will be liable**
> for any failures to honor the do-not-call request."

**⚠️ a consent duty hides inside the non-consent track.** to pass a suppression request to a partner:

> "must obtain a consumer's **prior express permission to share or forward** the consumer's request
> not to be called to a party other than the person or entity on whose behalf a call is made or an
> affiliated entity."

**⭐ affiliate reach turns on what the consumer would expect, not on your corporate chart.** standard 5:

> "In the absence of a specific request by the subscriber to the contrary, a residential subscriber's
> do-not-call request shall apply to the particular entity making the call (or on whose behalf a call
> is made), and will not apply to affiliated entities **unless the consumer reasonably would expect
> them to be included** given the identification of the caller and (for telemarketing calls) the
> product being advertised."

## ⭐ the two safe harbours are NOT the same, and one is narrower than it looks

this is the trap most likely to produce false confidence. there are **two** shelters, from different
sources, of different shapes, covering different things.

| | `§ 64.1200(c)(2)(i)` — the regulatory one | `§ 227(c)(5)` — the statutory one |
|---|---|---|
| **source** | the FCC's rule | the Act |
| **shape** | five enumerated standards | one sentence: *"reasonable practices and procedures"*, with due care |
| **what it answers** | liability for a call to a **registered** number | *"any action brought under this paragraph"* |
| **enumerated?** | ✅ yes — below | ⛔ **no** — the statute does not list what qualifies |

### the regulatory safe harbour's five standards

| # | standard | the operative requirement |
|---|----------|---------------------------|
| A | **written procedures** | *"established and implemented written procedures to comply with the national do-not-call rules"* |
| B | **training of personnel** | trained its own people *"and any entity assisting in its compliance"* |
| C | **recording** | *"maintained and recorded a list of telephone numbers that the seller may not contact"* |
| D | ⭐ **accessing the national do-not-call database** | a registry version obtained *"no more than **31 days** prior to the date any call is made"*, and *"maintains records documenting this process"* |
| E | **purchasing the national do-not-call database** | purchase access from the administrator, with no cost-share arrangement that divides access costs among client sellers |

⚠️ **in standard D the record is part of the standard, not evidence of it.** a 31-day-fresh scrub you
cannot document does not satisfy the text.

### ⛔ the scope limit a court stated plainly

`United States v. Dish Network` records that the two shelters do not cover the same calls:

> "The TSR safe harbor applied to Internal List Calls and Registry Calls. **The FCC Rule safe harbor
> only applied to Registry Calls.**"

⭐ **so an internal-list failure has no enumerated shelter.** if you fail to honor a request made
directly to you, the five-standard checklist above does not reach it — you are left with the
statutory *"due care"* sentence, which nobody has enumerated.

⚠️ **and note which way the contrast in that sentence runs.** the shelter `Dish` says *does* reach
internal-list calls is the **TSR's** — the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule at `16 C.F.R. § 310.4`,
a different regulator's rule under a different act. so the gap is not that no regulator wrote an
internal-list shelter; it is that **the one that did is not the FCC**, and this brief's whole program
is built on the FCC's rule. ⬜ **whether the TSR reaches you at all was not researched** — it turns
on whether your calls are *"telemarketing"* within the FTC's jurisdiction, which has carve-outs the
FCC's rule does not share. that is a question for counsel, and it is the first one to raise about
this gap.

## revocation — the same duty, and the trap inside it

`§ 64.1200(a)(10)` gives the honor deadline, and applies to this track expressly (it reaches calls
under *"paragraphs (a)(1) through (3) and (c)(2)"*):

> "All requests to revoke prior express consent or prior express written consent made in any
> reasonable manner must be honored within a reasonable time not to exceed **ten business days** from
> receipt of such request."

⛔ **and the provision most likely to break an otherwise careful program:**

> "Callers or senders of text messages … **may not designate an exclusive means** to request
> revocation of consent."

⭐ **so a program that accepts only literal `STOP`, or that routes revocation solely to a web form, is
non-compliant by construction** — not by a judgment call. the regulation names reply keywords
including *"stop"*, *"quit"*, *"end"*, *"revoke"*, *"opt out"*, *"cancel"*, and *"unsubscribe"* as
reasonable per se, but the list is a floor on what must be accepted, never a peak.

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| ⭐ does a consent record defend this track? | ⛔ **no** — `§ 227(c)(5)`'s defense has **no consent element**, and the subsection is framed around objection |
| does it matter how the call was dialed? | **no** — this track is dialer-agnostic; a manual live call can violate it |
| is one call actionable? | **no** — the right needs *"more than one telephone call within any 12-month period"* |
| ⭐ what is the payoff for the work? | the statute names the program itself an **affirmative defense** — the only track in this subject where that is true |
| is a written program enough? | ⛔ **no** — the text says *"established **and** implemented, with due care"* |
| are the two safe harbours the same? | ⭐ **no** — per `Dish`, *"The FCC Rule safe harbor only applied to Registry Calls"* |
| what has **no** enumerated shelter? | an **internal-list** failure |
| how fresh must the registry copy be? | *"no more than **31 days** prior to the date any call is made"*, with records that document the process |
| how long does a request last? | **5 years** for an internal request |
| does a request reach affiliates? | ⭐ **not by default** — only where *"the consumer reasonably would expect them to be included"* |
| may we forward a suppression request to a partner? | ⚠️ **only with the consumer's prior express permission** |
| who is liable if a vendor keeps the list? | *"the person or entity on whose behalf the call is made **will be liable**"* |
| may we require one opt-out channel? | ⛔ **no** — a caller *"may not designate an exclusive means"* |

## the honest gap

- ⛔ **`§ 227(c)(5)`'s "due care" is unconstrued here.** the defense's text is quoted; **no decision
  that applies the standard was read.** so what a court demands of a program is unknown — which is
  uncomfortable, because this is the layer whose whole value is that defense.
- ⚠️ **the residential/wireless boundary is named and not answered.** `(d)` is written to a
  *"residential telephone subscriber"*, and whether these duties reach a wireless number was not
  researched. this is the largest scope gap on this layer.
- ⬜ **state suppression duties are out of scope, and one concrete example shows why that matters.**
  this brief is federal only. `Fla. Stat. § 501.059` is a state analogue with its **own** suppression
  and cure machinery — a separate regime, not a restatement of the federal one — so a program that
  satisfies `§ 64.1200(d)` in full is **not thereby** compliant in Florida. ⛔ **and Florida is one
  state of fifty.** the sweep of the other forty-nine is not done, and no re-read of this brief
  substitutes for it.
- ⬜ **no decision was read on what satisfies standard 2 (training) or standard 4 (identification).**
  the text is quoted; its application is unresearched.
- ⚠️ **`Dish` is one case and its safe-harbour comparison is descriptive of that record.** it is the
  clearest statement found that the two shelters differ in scope; it is not a holding that they
  always do.
