# hazard.badge-implies-endorsement

## .what

this is the hazard the **entity-grain answer created**. right of publicity collapses at entity grain
in all five swept states — but the layer does not vanish, it is **replaced**. entities hold
**trademark**, so a badge rendered beside a company's name risks a claim that the publisher implied
an **affiliation** or an **endorsement** that does not exist.

⭐ the operative statutory words are, almost verbatim, a description of what a badge does:

> `[observed]` **§ 1125(a)(1)(A)** — *"is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to
> deceive as to the **affiliation, connection, or association** of such person with another person,
> or as to the **origin, sponsorship, or approval** of his or her goods, services, or commercial
> activities by another person"*

a badge is a sponsorship-and-approval signal by construction. that is its whole job.

## ⭐ .the two subsections are NOT symmetric — and the natural read gets it backwards

⚠️ **the intuitive answer here is wrong, and it is wrong in the dangerous direction.** asked whether a
directory may name the businesses it lists, most readers reach for *"nominative fair use"* and stop.
the statutory text shows a sharper and worse fact: **there IS a statutory fair-use exclusion, and it
does not apply to the subsection that reaches an ordinary listed business.**

| | **(a) false association** | **(c) dilution** |
|---|---|---|
| what it reaches | *"affiliation, connection, or association … sponsorship, or approval"* | *"dilution by blurring or dilution by tarnishment"* |
| **fame required?** | ⛔ **no** | ✅ yes — *"a **famous** mark that is distinctive"* |
| **statutory nominative-fair-use exclusion?** | ⛔ **NONE** | ✅ `[observed]` § 1125(c)(3)(A) |
| **statutory news exclusion?** | ⛔ **NONE** | ✅ `[observed]` § 1125(c)(3)(B) |
| **statutory noncommercial exclusion?** | ⛔ **NONE** | ✅ `[observed]` § 1125(c)(3)(C) |
| who may sue | *"any person who believes that he or she is or is likely to be damaged"* | *"the owner of a famous mark"* |

`[observed]` the exclusions are written **inside subsection (c)**: *"The following shall not be
actionable as dilution by blurring or dilution by tarnishment **under this subsection**: (A) Any fair
use, **including a nominative or descriptive fair use** … (B) All forms of news reporting and news
commentary. (C) Any noncommercial use of a mark."*

⭐⭐ **so the three defenses a directory would reach for first are all statutory — and all confined to
the subsection that does NOT reach an ordinary contractor.** for § 1125(a), nominative fair use is
`[unverified]` **case law** (the doctrine exists; the case law was not captured), and there is no
statutory news or noncommercial carve-out at all.

### and the asymmetry runs the wrong way for a directory

⛔ **(c) reaches only famous marks. (a) reaches everyone.** so the exposure that **scales with
coverage** — one claim per listed business — is the one with no statutory defense. dilution, which
has three, is the one that will almost never apply to a local plumber.

⚠️ **so a reader who treats the Lanham layer as "mostly answered by nominative fair use" has it
inverted.** the statutory text answers it *for the case a local directory does not face*, and is
silent on the case it does. **check which subsection a defense sits in before it is leaned on.**

## ⭐ .and § 230 does not reach it either

`[observed]` **47 U.S.C. § 230(e)(2)**: the shield expressly preserves **intellectual-property** law.
so the § 230 answer that governs review content does **not** reach a false-association claim about
the directory's own badge.

that matters because the badge is **the publisher's speech, not the subject's**. under
`define.boundary.who-speaks-decides-the-regime.[lesson].md`, § 230 asks *who authored* — and the
publisher authored the badge. so even without § 230(e)(2), the publisher is the information content
provider here. **two independent reasons the shield is absent.** this is the sharpest single point in
the layer.

## ⭐ .the asymmetric-disclosure rule points the OTHER way here

this is the one place in the whole design where `rule.require.asymmetric-disclosure` **helps for the
opposite reason**.

| layer | which state is risky |
|---|---|
| right of publicity | the **name on the page** — the badge's content is irrelevant |
| stale public record | the ⭐ **favorable** badge — a positive that decayed becomes a false statement |
| ⭐ **false association** | the ⭐ **favorable** badge again — a positive is what implies approval |
| | `⬜ unknown` asserts no relationship at all, so it cannot imply one |

⭐ **so the rule that governs publication also bounds this hazard** — and it bounds it on the *same*
side as staleness. `⬜` is inert on all three layers. the exposure concentrates, again, on the
**favorable** badge — the third distinct body of law to land on that same asymmetry.

## .the two directions of confusion

`[inferred]` the statute reaches confusion *"as to the affiliation … or as to the origin,
sponsorship, or approval of **his or her** goods, services, or commercial activities **by another
person**"* — which runs in two directions, and both are live:

| direction | the claim a plaintiff argues |
|---|---|
| **the subject endorses the directory** | *"this contractor is a partner of this directory"* — the classic listed-business claim. sharpened enormously by any **paid** placement |
| **the directory certifies the subject** | *"this directory is an authorized verifier of licenses"* — sharpened by the word **verified** itself, and by any seal-like or crest-like badge shape |

⚠️ `[inferred]` the second direction is the one a badge vocabulary invites. the glossary rule in this
cluster already requires that **`verified` never appear alone — it always qualifies the noun it
confirmed** (`verified license`, not `verified`). that rule was adopted for `rule.require.ubiqlang`
reasons. ⭐ **it turns out to carry a second, unplanned benefit here:** *"verified license"* names a
**record read off a registry**; a bare *"Verified"* seal names a **status conferred by the
publisher**. the ubiqlang discipline was already the Lanham mitigation.

## .the exposure, by what raises it

| axis | what raises it | tag |
|---|---|---|
| **their logo or mark rendered** | the statute reaches *"any word, term, name, symbol, or device"* — a plain name is covered too, but a **logo** moves the confusion analysis sharply | `[observed]` on scope, `[inferred]` on weight |
| ⛔ **paid placement** | a purchased rank or position is an affiliation **in fact**, which makes the inference true rather than confused | `[inferred]` |
| **seal-shaped badge design** | a crest, ribbon, or checkmark reads as a conferred certification | `[inferred]` |
| **the word "verified" unqualified** | names a status the publisher granted rather than a record it read | `[inferred]` |
| **absence of a disclaimer of affiliation** | the one place a disclaimer genuinely does work — see below | `[inferred]` |

## ⭐ .the disclaimer works HERE — and only here

⛔ across every other layer in this directory, a disclaimer was listed under *what does not mitigate*:
it does not cure an FCRA furnishment, it does not cure a right-of-publicity use, it does not cure a
stale false statement.

⭐ **false association is the exception, and the reason is structural:** the claim's element is
**confusion**, and a disclaimer of affiliation goes directly to whether a consumer would be confused.
it is not a waiver bolted onto a violation; it is evidence against an element.

`[inferred]` so *"not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the businesses listed"* is
low-cost and does real work here, where the same sentence is decorative elsewhere. ⚠️ but it is
evidence, not immunity — a disclaimer does not rescue a **paid** placement, because there the
affiliation is real.

## .the mitigations, ranked

`[inferred]`:

1. ⛔ **no paid placement** — the only lever that makes the implied affiliation **true**. structural,
   and it is the same #1 as the publicity layer.
   ⭐ **cited, not restated: `rule.require.asymmetric-disclosure` § paid placement is canonical** and
   scopes it to **any status cell, badge, or rank position, on any page, at any grain**.
2. ⭐ **badge language names the record, not the publisher's judgment** — *"license #12345 active on the state
   registry as of 2026-03-01"* rather than *"Verified Pro"*. this is `rule.require.ubiqlang` already.
3. **plain-text name only; never render their logo or mark**.
4. **a persistent disclaimer of affiliation**, placed where the badge is — the one layer where it
   works.
5. **badge shape is informational, not seal-like** — a data row, not a crest.
6. **`⬜ unknown` renders identically for a non-participant** — it asserts no relationship, so it
   cannot imply one.

⛔ **what does NOT mitigate it:** § 230 (`[observed]` § 230(e)(2), and the publisher authored the
badge); the statutory fair-use, news, and noncommercial exclusions (`[observed]` all three are
dilution-only); the subject's non-participation (a claim under (a) needs no relationship at all).

## .the test

> **"would a consumer read this badge as a relationship between the directory and the subject —
> rather than as a fact read off a public record?"**

- it names a record, with a source and a date → the fact frame holds
- it names a status the publisher appears to confer (*"Verified"*, *"Certified"*, a seal) → ⚠️ the
  relationship read is live
- position or rank was **purchased** → ⛔ the relationship is real, and no disclaimer reaches it

## .enforcement

> ⭐ **these are DERIVED checks, not original mandates** — each says what to verify so this body of
> law is not tripped. the convention is declared in `hazard._.catalogue` § *the convention*; the
> original mandates live in `rule.*` files. **a `[hazard]` that enforces is checkable, not normative.**

- **paid** placement = **blocker** — ⭐ scope owned by `rule.require.asymmetric-disclosure` § paid
  placement (**any cell, any page, any grain**); *here* it matters because the affiliation becomes
  **true**, and truth is what removes the confusion defense
- a badge that renders the subject's **logo or mark** = **blocker**
- a bare *"Verified"* / *"Certified"* label with no qualified noun = **blocker**
  (`rule.require.ubiqlang` + this layer, for the same reason)
- a seal-, crest-, or shield-shaped badge = **blocker**
- reliance on **§ 230** as the answer to a false-association claim = **blocker** (§ 230(e)(2), and
  the publisher authored the badge)
- reliance on the § 1125(c)(3) **news** or **noncommercial** exclusions against an (a) claim =
  **blocker** (they are dilution-only)
- absence of an affiliation disclaimer on a badge surface = **nitpick** (evidence, not immunity)

## ⭐⭐ .the sole-proprietor overlap — one badge, TWO claims

⚠️ **this brief is framed at entity grain, and for this trade that frame is incomplete.**
`define.boundary.entity-grain-vs-person-grain` names the sole proprietor and the DBA as *"the normal
case, not the edge"* in the contractor trade — a business whose name **is** a natural person's name.

⛔ **so a single badge beside "J. Smith Electric" can carry two independent claims at once:**

| claim | who brings it | what the badge did |
|---|---|---|
| **false association** — 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a) | the **mark owner** whose seal was reproduced | implied an affiliation or approval that does not exist |
| **right of publicity** — the five state statutes | ⭐ **the natural person** whose name the business name *is* | used their name for a commercial purpose without consent |

⭐ **the five, named rather than gestured at** — each reaches a *natural person*, which is exactly why
a sole proprietor's business name puts the person back inside a statute the entity frame had exited:

| state | the words that reach the person | verdict at entity grain |
|---|---|---|
| **FL** | `[observed]` § 540.08(1) — *"any **natural person**"* | ⛔ vacates |
| **IN** | `[observed]` § 32-36-1-6 — *"'personality' means a **living or deceased natural person**"* | ⛔ vacates |
| **TX** | `[observed]` Tex. Prop. Code § 26.002 — *"An **individual** has a property right in the use of the individual's name"* | ⛔ vacates |
| **CA** | `[observed]` § 3344(a)(1) — *"in the case of a **minor**, the prior consent of their parent or legal guardian"* | ⛔ vacates `[inferred]` |
| **NY** | `[observed]` § 51 — *"Any **person** whose name … is used within this state"* | ⛔ vacates `[inferred]` |

⛔ **so the ROP layer vacates at entity grain in all five — and that is precisely the reason THIS
brief exists.** false association is what fills the space the publicity statutes leave. ⚠️ **the
vacancy reverses for a sole proprietor**, and that reversal is the overlap this section is about.

⭐ **and the two do not share a defense.** consent from the mark owner does no work against the ROP
claim; consent from the person does no work against the false-association claim. **each must be
cleared on its own track**, and a mitigation aimed at one leaves the other fully live.

⚠️ **the practical tell: entity grain does NOT reliably exit this brief's overlap.** the collapse that
works elsewhere in the cluster — *the subject of record is a business, so the person-grain statutes do
not reach it* — fails exactly where the business name and the person's name are the same string. see
`hazard.unclaimed-profile-right-of-publicity` for the ROP track and
`define.boundary.entity-grain-vs-person-grain` § *the leak paths*.

## ⚠️ .what is NOT covered here

- ⚠️ `[unverified]` **nominative fair use under § 1125(a)** — the doctrine exists in case law; the
  case law was **not captured**. it is flagged rather than relied upon, and it is the single largest
  open question in this layer.
- ⚠️ `[unverified]` **state unfair-competition and deceptive-trade-practices analogues.**
  `rule.require.five-state-baseline` governs **state-law** briefs; § 1125 is federal, so the rule
  does not bind this brief — but the state analogues are a real, un-swept parallel layer.
- ⚠️ `[unverified]` **§ 1114** (registered-mark infringement) was not captured; this brief reaches
  only § 1125.
