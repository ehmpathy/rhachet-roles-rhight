# hazard.cashout-money-transmitter-license.CONDITIONAL

## .what

money-transmitter-license risk for a rewards program is **conditional, not inherent**. money
transmission attaches only when an actor does **two** things together: **accept** value from one person
**and** transmit that value **to another** (a third party). a reward point can be structured so neither
element is present on the paths that matter:

- **an earned point**, never sold, loaded, or deposited — so no value is accepted from a holder
- **a closed-loop consumer redemption** — a point buys only an in-program purchase, never third-party cash
- **an earned cash-out paid from the issuer's own revenue** — the issuer pays down its own booked debt
  (accounts-payable), it does not move a customer's funds to a third party

on those facts the regime does **not** attach. it attaches where either element becomes present (the
flip-triggers live in the companion stored-value brief).

## .why

- the risk rides on **two conjunctive elements** — accept-from-one **and** transmit-to-another. break
  either element and the definition fails to close.
- an earned, closed-loop, own-debt structure breaks **both**: no acceptance step (the point is earned),
  no transmission to a third party (the consumer path is closed-loop; the cash-out is an own-debt
  paydown).
- indiana and florida illustrate the range. indiana supplies unusually clean text — its
  money-transmission statute writes a loyalty / rewards / promotional carve-out and a closed-loop
  carve-out **into the definition of stored value**.
- florida is an example of a stricter state — its stored-value definition is broad and carries **no
  express loyalty carve-out**, so under a statute like FL's the analysis leans on the two-element
  transmitter test plus a strictly closed-loop, non-transferable consumer point.
- the **agent-of-payee** exemption is a backstop where a collection step ever appears.

## the two-element test verbatim

federal law builds the regulated service on **acceptance** plus **transmission** — two elements that
must both be present:

> "acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and
> the transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another
> location or person by any means"

**source**: [31 CFR § 1010.100(ff)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)

the same rule carves out value moved as a mere incident of a person's **own** sale of goods or
services — the exemption that fits an operator who pays its own obligations:

> "Accepts and transmits funds only integral to the sale of goods or the provision of services, other
> than money transmission services"

**source**: [31 CFR § 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii)(F)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)

the federal registration statute frames the covered business the same way — a business in the
**transmission** of value that substitutes for currency:

> "any other person who engages as a business in the transmission of currency, funds, or value that
> substitutes for currency"

**source**: [31 U.S.C. § 5330(d)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5330)

florida's transmitter definition needs an **intermediary** that receives value to move it from one
person to another — a third-party transfer, not a debtor who pays its own debt:

> "a corporation, limited liability company, limited liability partnership, or foreign entity
> qualified to do business in this state which receives currency, monetary value, a payment
> instrument, or virtual currency for the purpose of acting as an intermediary to transmit currency,
> monetary value, a payment instrument, or virtual currency from one person to another location or
> person by any means"

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 560.103(24)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/560.103)

## the element table — when each element is absent

| # | element the regime needs | earned consumer point | earned cash-out |
|---|--------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **accept** value from one person | no — the point is earned, never loaded or deposited | no — the payee earned it for a service; no funds were accepted to hold |
| 2 | **transmit** it to another person | no — closed-loop; the point buys only an in-program purchase | no — the issuer's **own** revenue pays down its **own** payable, not a third-party transfer |
| verdict | both elements required (conjunctive) | **outside** | **outside** |

either absent element defeats the definition. on these facts **both** are absent on **both** paths.

## an illustrative authority — indiana writes the carve-out into the definition

indiana's money-transmission modernization act removes loyalty and closed-loop value **from the
definition of stored value itself** — an unusually clean example of the carve-out in statutory text:

> "'Stored value' means monetary value representing a claim, against the issuer, that is evidenced by
> an electronic or digital record and that is intended and accepted for use as a means of redemption
> for money or monetary value, or payment for goods or services. The term includes 'prepaid access'
> as defined in 31 CFR 1010.100. The term does not include: (A) a payment instrument; (B) closed loop
> stored value; or (C) stored value not sold to the public but issued and distributed as part of a
> loyalty, rewards, or promotional program."

**source**: [Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-201](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-28-financial-institutions/in-code-sect-28-8-4-1-201/)

an earned, non-transferable, closed-loop consumer point maps onto **both** exclusions (B) and (C): it is
closed-loop **and** it is a rewards-program value not sold to the public.

the same act supplies the **agent-of-payee** backstop — a person appointed to collect a payment on a
payee's behalf is not a transmitter when payment to the agent counts as payment to the payee:

> "Payment for the goods and services is treated as received by the payee upon receipt by the agent"

**source**: [Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-301](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-28-financial-institutions/in-code-sect-28-8-4-1-301/)

the stakes if the design ever flips into the regime — indiana bars unlicensed transmission outright:

> "A person may not: (1) engage in the business of money transmission; or (2) advertise, solicit, or
> hold itself out as providing money transmission; unless the person is licensed under this chapter."

**source**: [Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-501](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-28-financial-institutions/in-code-sect-28-8-4-1-501/)

## severity

**conditional** — **outside on the earned/closed-loop/own-debt facts**, **blocker if any of those
facts changes**.

the risk is dormant while the three facts hold (earned-only, closed-loop consumer redemption, cash-out
from the issuer's own revenue). florida illustrates the downside a stricter state can impose where a
change lands a program inside the regime — unlicensed money-services activity is a **felony**, scaled by
value:

> "A person may not engage in the business of a money services business or deferred presentment
> provider in this state unless the person is licensed or exempted from licensure under this chapter."

and the penalty tiers bite by transaction value: a third-degree felony at $300–$19,999, a
second-degree felony at $20,000–$99,999, and a first-degree felony at $100,000 or more.

**source**: [Fla. Stat. § 560.125](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/560.125)

so the line is: on the earned/closed-loop/own-debt facts the regime does not attach; where a fact
changes, the exposure is criminal in FL plus federal registration under 31 U.S.C. § 5330.

## the five-state carve-out matrix

per `rule.require.five-state-baseline`, the sweep across TX, IN, FL, CA, NY turns on one question:
**does the state write an express loyalty / closed-loop carve-out into its stored-value definition?**
three of the five do; two do not — and the two that do not set the baseline.

| state | express loyalty / closed-loop carve-out? | verbatim-cited authority | strict? |
|-------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------|---------|
| TX | **yes** — MTMA excludes closed-loop + loyalty/rewards/promotional stored value | Tex. Fin. Code § 152.003 | |
| IN | **yes** — closed-loop + loyalty/rewards/promotional excluded from "stored value" | Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-201 | |
| FL | **no** express loyalty carve-out — broad stored-value / transmitter net | Fla. Stat. § 560.103 | ⭐ (broad net) |
| CA | **yes** — loyalty/rewards/promotional + closed-loop excluded from "stored value" | Cal. Fin. Code § 2003 | |
| NY | **no** MTMA-style carve-out — a narrower **activity-based** license scope instead | N.Y. Banking Law §§ 640–641 | ⭐ (no carve-out) |

**baseline = do not depend on a statutory carve-out.** two of the five (FL, NY) lack the express
loyalty carve-out that TX, IN, and CA supply — FL via a broad stored-value net, NY via an activity-
based scope with no stored-value exclusion list. so the strictest-common-denominator design must
survive the **two-element transmitter test itself** (accept-from-one + transmit-to-another), which the
earned / closed-loop / own-debt structure does in all five states — rather than lean on a carve-out
that two states do not offer.

the CA and TX carve-outs, quoted verbatim, show the language the two carve-out states share. CA:

> "Stored value not sold to the public but issued and distributed as part of a loyalty, rewards, or
> promotional program."

**source**: [Cal. Fin. Code § 2003 (FindLaw)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/financial-code/fin-sect-2003/)

TX (the Money Services Modernization Act), whose "stored value" term "does not include … closed-loop
stored value, or stored value not sold to the public but issued and distributed as part of a loyalty,
rewards, or promotional program":

**source**: [Tex. Fin. Code § 152.003 (FindLaw)](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/finance-code/fin-sect-152-003/)

NY, by contrast, licenses an **activity** — no stored-value exclusion list to fall inside. verbatim on
the scope, N.Y. Banking Law § 641:

> "No person shall engage in the business of selling or issuing checks, or engage in the business of
> receiving money for transmission or transmitting the same, without a license therefor"

because NY's net is cast on the **transmission activity** rather than on a broad "stored value"
noun, an earned closed-loop point — which involves no receipt of money for transmission — is arguably
outside NY's scope on the activity test itself, even though NY writes no loyalty carve-out. that is the
nuance: NY is strict because it has **no carve-out to rely on**, but its narrower scope may not reach a
closed-loop earned point in the first place.

**source**: [N.Y. Banking Law § 641 (NYS Senate, official)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/BNK/641)
· [N.Y. Banking Law § 640 — definitions (NYS Senate, official)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/BNK/640)

## IN vs FL divergence — two representative examples

indiana and florida sit near opposite ends of the range and illustrate how the same fact pattern can
turn on a state's statutory text:

| dimension | indiana | florida |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| stored-value definition | carries **express** carve-outs: closed-loop **and** loyalty/rewards/promotional | broad; **no** express loyalty carve-out in the definitions |
| the transmitter test | two-element (accept + transmit) | two-element intermediary "from one person to another" |
| agent-of-payee backstop | codified (§ 28-8-4.1-301) | present in ch. 560's exemption scheme |
| unlicensed-activity edge | prohibition (§ 28-8-4.1-501) | **felony**, tiered by value (§ 560.125) |
| what the analysis leans on | the loyalty/closed-loop **definitional** carve-out | the two-element **transmitter** test + strictly closed-loop, **non-transferable** consumer point |

florida is broader on stored value, so a loyalty carve-out is not available there — under a statute like
that, the analysis rests on a strictly **closed-loop and non-transferable** consumer point and a
cash-out that is an own-payable paydown, so element 2 (transmit-to-another) is never present.

the divergence is not a one-state quirk — practitioner analysis of the model act confirms adoptions
diverge on exactly these carve-outs, so "managing compliance with money transmission laws generally
remains a state-by-state proposition for money transmitters."

**source**: [Cooley — US states adopt model money transmission act, but harmonization remains elusive](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2024/2024-08-20-us-states-adopt-model-money-transmission-act-but-harmonization-remains-elusive)

## the honest gap

no court has ruled that an earned-debt, closed-loop point escapes money transmission; the analysis is a
**text-level** read of the two-element test plus the IN carve-outs, strong but counsel-confirmable. two
soft edges:

- **the stricter-state silence (FL as example).** a state like florida whose stored-value definition
  has no loyalty carve-out throws the analysis back onto the transmitter test itself — which holds only
  while the consumer point stays closed-loop and non-transferable. a transferable or cash-redeemable
  point under such a statute loses the cleanest defense.
- **an earned cash-out is a real payout.** it falls outside where the issuer's **own** revenue settles a
  payable it **owes** for a service — not a customer's funds forwarded to a third party. that boundary
  is where the analysis turns (see the debt-anchor brief).

## key takeaways

| question | answer |
|----------|--------|
| is money-transmitter risk inherent? | no — **conditional**; it needs accept-from-one **and** transmit-to-another |
| why is an earned/closed-loop point outside? | the point is earned (no accept), the consumer path is closed-loop, the cash-out is an own-debt paydown (no third-party transmit) |
| an illustrative clean authority? | Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-201 — loyalty/rewards + closed-loop carved out of "stored value" |
| the backstop? | agent-of-payee (Ind. Code § 28-8-4.1-301) |
| an example of a stricter state? | **florida** — broad stored value, no express loyalty carve-out |
| what does the analysis rest on under a statute like FL's? | a strictly closed-loop **and** non-transferable consumer point |
| how bad if an element becomes present? | FL unlicensed activity is a felony (§ 560.125); federal registration under 31 U.S.C. § 5330 |
