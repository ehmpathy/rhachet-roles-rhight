# rule.prefer.verified-disclosed-sentiment-blind-reviews

## .what

an incentivized review sits inside three **independent** FTC prohibitions at once; each must be
cleared on its own, and a pass on one does not carry a pass on another:

- **(1) the VERIFIED line** — a review tied to a real, completed transaction is outside the
  fake-review ban; a fabricated one is inside it (16 CFR § 465.2).
- **(2) the DISCLOSURE line** — a material connection surfaced clearly and conspicuously on the review
  is disclosed; one left hidden is an undisclosed-incentive wrong (16 CFR § 255.5).
- **(3) the SENTIMENT line** — a reward blind to tenor is outside the sentiment ban; a reward
  conditioned on a particular sentiment is a per-se violation (16 CFR § 465.4).

and note the sharp part: **disclosure does NOT cure a sentiment gate.** a reward conditioned on a
positive review is a per-se § 465.4 violation **even if disclosed**. the three lines are separate; a
program is lawful only where it clears all three, and unlawful the moment it fails any one.

## .why

the FTC 2024 review rule — 16 CFR Part 465, in effect since 2024-10-21 — makes fake / unverified
reviews, undisclosed material connections, and sentiment-conditioned incentives violations that
carry civil penalties (up to ~$51,744 per violation). the three lines map one-to-one onto the three
violations, and each is cleared or crossed on its own facts:

- **verified vs. fake** (§ 465.2): a reward tied to a real, completed transaction is outside the fake-review
  ban; a reward for a fabricated review is inside it.
- **disclosed vs. undisclosed** (§ 255.5): a clear-and-conspicuous disclosure on the review is
  outside the undisclosed-incentive wrong; a hidden connection is inside it.
- **sentiment-blind vs. sentiment-gated** (§ 465.4): a reward paid for **any** honest review — a rave
  or a complaint alike — is outside the sentiment ban; a reward priced to praise is inside it.

the three lines are cumulative: a review is lawful only where it sits on the outside of all three,
and unlawful where it falls inside any one. only the fake, the hidden, and the praise-gated are what
the rule targets.

## the three lines

each line below carries the verbatim FTC text it rests on.

### line 1 — VERIFIED vs. fake (§ 465.2)

a review is outside the fake-review ban **only** where it sits behind a real, completed transaction. 16 CFR
§ 465.2(a) names the three misrepresentations that make a review fake / false:

> "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to write,
> create, or sell a consumer review, consumer testimonial, or celebrity testimonial that materially
> misrepresents, expressly or by implication:
> (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists;
> (2) That the reviewer or testimonialist used or otherwise had experience with the product,
> service, or business that is the subject of the review or testimonial; or
> (3) The reviewer's or testimonialist's experience with the product, service, or business that is
> the subject of the review or testimonial."

**source**: [16 CFR § 465.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.2)

a verify-before-reward step targets all three prongs at once: it confirms the reviewer is a real
person (prong 1) who had a real, completed transaction (prong 2) and can speak to a real experience (prong
3). the rule's reach is broad — § 465.1 defines a consumer review to include a **purported**
consumer's evaluation:

> "A consumer's evaluation, or a purported consumer's evaluation, of a product, service, or business
> that is submitted by the consumer or purported consumer and that is published to a website or
> platform dedicated in whole or in part to receiving and displaying such evaluations."

**source**: [16 CFR § 465.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.1)

because a **purported** reviewer is in scope, the line is not "did someone post this" but "was there
a real, verified transaction behind it."

### line 2 — DISCLOSED vs. undisclosed (§ 255.5)

a reward for a verified transaction is a **material connection** between the reviewer and the seller. 16 CFR
§ 255.5 states the duty:

> "When there exists a connection between the endorser and the seller of the advertised product that
> might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not
> reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously."

and it names the reward forms that count:

> "They can include monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products (including
> products unrelated to the endorsed product) to an endorser, regardless of whether the advertiser
> requires an endorsement in return."

**source**: [16 CFR § 255.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5)

"clear and conspicuous" is not a loose standard. § 465.1 defines it, and sets the bar for a digital
surface where a customer decides:

> "easily noticeable (i.e., difficult to miss) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers."

> "A disclosure is not clear and conspicuous if a consumer must take any action, such as clicking on
> a hyperlink or hovering over an icon, to see it."

**source**: [16 CFR § 465.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.1)

so a badge or a line of text on the review itself clears the bar; a reward buried behind a "learn
more" link does not.

### line 3 — SENTIMENT-BLIND vs. sentiment-gated (§ 465.4)

a reward is outside this ban only where it pays for a review of **any** tenor — a five-star rave and
a one-star complaint earn the same reward. 16 CFR § 465.4 bans a sentiment-conditioned incentive per
se:

> "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to
> provide compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or conditioned expressly or by
> implication on, the writing or creation of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment,
> whether positive or negative, regarding the product, service, or business that is the subject of
> the review."

**source**: [16 CFR § 465.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.4)

two features make this the sharpest of the three lines: **"whether positive or negative"** (the ban
reaches any target sentiment, not just praise) and **"expressly or by implication"** (a design that
never says "5 stars = reward" but pays only when a review reads well can still trip it). the lawful
path stays open only for an incentive **not** conditioned on sentiment. per a national law review
analysis:

> "The Rule does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there is not an express or
> implied requirement that the reviews have to express a particular sentiment."

**source**: [national law review — fifteen things advertisers need to know about the ftc's consumer reviews and testimonials rule](https://natlawreview.com/article/what-are-fifteen-things-advertisers-need-know-about-ftcs-consumer-reviews-and)

## disclosure cures § 255.5 but NOT § 465.4

this is the key distinction, and the one most easily missed: the two duties are **distinct**, and a
cure for one is not a cure for the other.

| wrong | the cure | does disclosure cure it? |
|-------|----------|--------------------------|
| **undisclosed** material connection (§ 255.5) | a clear-and-conspicuous disclosure on the review | **yes** — disclosure is the cure |
| **sentiment-gated** reward (§ 465.4) | make the reward blind to tenor — a structural fix | **NO** — the sentiment condition is itself the violation |

a morgan lewis analysis states the prohibited object of § 465.4 verbatim — an incentive conditioned
on a particular sentiment — which by its terms is a wrong about the **condition**, not about
disclosure:

> "prohibits businesses from providing compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or
> conditioned on (either expressly or by implication), the writing or creation of consumer reviews
> that express a *particular sentiment*"

**source**: [morgan lewis — ftc issues final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials)

the practical lesson: a reward that pays only for a praise review is a **per-se** § 465.4 violation
**even if** the reward is disclosed clearly and conspicuously. surfaced or hidden, a reward priced to
sentiment is prohibited. disclosure fixes the § 255.5 line; only a **sentiment-blind** design fixes
the § 465.4 line. both must hold at once.

the two lines are drawn separately in
`define.boundary.disclosed-vs-undisclosed-incentive.[lesson].md` (the § 255.5 cure) and
`define.boundary.sentiment-blind-vs-sentiment-gated.[lesson].md` (the § 465.4 no-cure).

## the statutory teeth

part 465 is a trade-regulation rule under the FTC act; a violation carries civil penalties. 15 U.S.C.
§ 45(a)(1) declares the root wrong unlawful, and § 45(m)(1)(A) supplies the penalty hook:

> "Unfair methods of competition in or affecting commerce, and unfair or deceptive acts or practices
> in or affecting commerce, are hereby declared unlawful."

> "The Commission may commence a civil action to recover a civil penalty in a district court of the
> United States against any person, partnership, or corporation which violates any rule under this
> subchapter respecting unfair or deceptive acts or practices"

**source**: [15 U.S.C. § 45 — ftc act § 5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45)

a law-firm summary states the effective date and the per-violation penalty cap:

> "The Rule will become effective on October 21, 2024."

> "The Final Rule additionally authorizes the FTC to seek civil penalties of up to $51,744 per
> violation"

and the breadth of the fake-review ban:

> "The Final Rule forbids businesses from creating, buying, selling or disseminating fake or false
> reviews or testimonials"

**source**: [goodwin — ftc finalizes rule on consumer reviews](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/09/alerts-practices-cldr-ftc-finalizes-rule-on-consumer-reviews)

## severity

**prefer → nitpick.** these three lines are the terrain, not a single mandated route — the law does
not force one specific program shape, and counsel may clear a given design on its own facts. but each
line is **load-critical**: a program on the outside of all three is lawful, and one that falls inside
any one is not.

**the stakes:** a review that sits outside all three lines is outside the FTC review-rule violations
— the fake-review ban (§ 465.2), the undisclosed-material-connection wrong (§ 255.5), and the
sentiment-gate per-se violation (§ 465.4) — and thereby out of reach of the
**~$51,744-per-violation** civil-penalty exposure the rule carries. a failure on any one line
re-opens exactly the violation that line governs: an unverified reward invites a fake-review claim,
an undisclosed reward invites a § 255.5 claim, and a praise-gated reward is a per-se § 465.4
violation that no disclosure saves.

## the honest gap

- **state incentivized-review law may add on top.** these three lines are the **federal** terrain;
  a state may impose its own duties on incentivized or disclosed reviews that counsel must check
  separately.
- **the merit-vs-paid-placement edge is a separate question.** how a verified, disclosed,
  sentiment-blind review is **ranked or surfaced** (paid placement, order of display) is governed by
  other rules — including § 465.7 review-suppression — and is not settled by these three lines.
- **no case law applies part 465 to a "reward for a verified transaction" review.** the text
  and the law-firm guides are strong, but the specific application is counsel-confirmable; the rule
  is recent (effective 2024-10-21) and its enforcement history is thin.
- **"clear and conspicuous" is a standard, not a template**, and **"by implication"** in § 465.4 is a
  fact question — a reward nominally blind but paid only after a human reads the review for tone could
  read as a hidden sentiment gate. counsel must review the actual pay-out mechanic.
- **the $51,744 figure is inflation-adjusted** and rests on law-firm summaries (the ftc.gov primary
  pages 403-block automated fetch); verify the current per-violation cap before any reliance.
