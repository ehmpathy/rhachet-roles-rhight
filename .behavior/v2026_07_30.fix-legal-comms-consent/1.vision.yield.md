## the outcome world

### before

the human holds the risk as **folklore**. "you can get sued for a text to a customer" is a rumor with
no shape: no number attached, no test for which messages are exposed, no idea whether the company's
extant insurance answers it. every decision about outbound phone contact is made on vibes, and the
two failure modes are symmetric and both expensive:

- **over-fear** — the company throttles or kills a lawful channel it was entitled to use
- **under-fear** — the company sends at volume on consent it cannot later produce, and discovers the
  gap when a demand letter arrives with a class definition attached

### after

the `counselor` role carries a `comms/` brief family the same shape as its extant `market/` family.
the folklore resolves into four answerable questions, each with a cited redline:

| the question | resolves to |
|--------------|-------------|
| *can we call/text this person?* | a boundary test on four axes: message purpose, dialer type, number type, served state |
| *what is it worth if we're wrong?* | a per-message, uncapped, trebled-on-willful figure — scaled as a class, not as a single call |
| *can we prove it?* | a consent-record schema: what fields, captured when, retained how long, producible per-number |
| *are we covered?* | a coverage map that starts from "your CGL names the TCPA as an exclusion by name" |

### the two "aha" moments

**aha #1 — the damages are per-message, uncapped, and the plaintiff's bar knows it.**
`47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3)` gives *"$500 in damages for each such violation"*, trebled to $1,500 on a
willful or knowing finding. there is no statutory cap. the exposure is not $500 — it is $500 ×
(messages × recipients). a campaign to 40,000 numbers is a nominal $20M demand before the first
argument about whether the conduct was willful. **this is why the noise the human heard exists**: the
statute is built to aggregate, so the plaintiff's bar aggregates.

⚠️ **the mitigant this claim omitted** (hidden assumption **H9**): the multiplier assumes **class
treatment is available**. an enforceable **arbitration clause with a class waiver** is the standard
corporate defense against exactly this aggregation, and the first draft never mentioned it. the
per-message math is right; whether it ever reaches $20M depends on a term that may already sit in the
company's own terms of service. **this is the largest single caveat on the vision's lead result** and
belongs high in the research load.

**aha #2 — the reflex "we have general liability insurance" deserves a hard check, because the ISO
form excludes the TCPA *by name*.** the ISO Commercial General Liability form `CG 00 01 04 13` does
not merely fail to reach TCPA claims by accident; it excludes them in an exclusion that **names the
TCPA in its first enumerated clause**, under *both* Coverage A and Coverage B. "we carry GL" feels
like an answer and, against this form, is not one.

⚠️ **scoped honestly** (see U4/U5): the form *text* is verbatim-verified. that this edition is
*current*, that ISO forms are what a given carrier actually issued, and whether the exclusion can be
bought back by endorsement are all **unverified**. so the finding is "check your own policy for this
exclusion title", not "you are uncovered."

the two together reframe the risk: **an uncapped, aggregate liability that sits outside the policy
most companies assume covers it.** that is the shape of the thing, and it is the payload of the
deliverable.

**aha #3 — the statute contains its own safe harbor, and the compliance program *is* the defense.**
⭐ *added after the citations were re-pulled off official sources; it was invisible to the first pass.*

`47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5)` closes with a sentence the earlier research never surfaced:

> "It shall be an affirmative defense in any action brought under this paragraph that the defendant
> has established and implemented, with due care, reasonable practices and procedures to effectively
> prevent telephone solicitations in violation of the regulations prescribed under this subsection."

Florida writes a second off-ramp of a different kind: `§ 501.059(10)(c)` makes a `STOP` reply a
**condition precedent** to any damages action over texts, and gives the sender **15 days to cure**.

this inverts the deliverable's posture. the first two ahas describe a company as a target. this one
says the statute **names the shield** — a documented, exercised compliance program is not merely
prudence, it is a **pleaded defense on the do-not-call track**, and one with no consent element at
all. so the runbook question (`.what is awkward` #2) is not a nice-to-have adjacent to the legal
terrain — **the runbook is a statutory defense**, and that materially raises its value and changes
how it should be scoped.

⚠️ it also exposes a frame defect the vision carried from its own title (hidden assumption **H5**):
*"phone-consent liability"* pre-committed to consent as the whole story. it is not. one of the two
private rights of action turns on **suppression practices**, not consent.

## user experience

### the usecases

| # | usecase | the goal | the moment it is asked |
|---|---------|----------|------------------------|
| 1 | **pre-launch clearance** | "is this outbound campaign lawful, and is our consent provable?" | before a send |
| 2 | **exposure scale** | "a demand letter arrived — what is the realistic range?" | after a claim |
| 3 | **lead procurement diligence** | "we buy leads — does the seller's consent travel to us?" | at vendor intake |
| 4 | **coverage renewal** | "what do we ask our broker to add, and what will they say no to?" | at policy renewal |
| 5 | **channel design** | "what does the opt-in form and the STOP handler have to do?" | at build time |
| 6 | **retrospective triage** ⚠️ | "we **already sent** 500k texts on consent we cannot produce — what now?" | after the worry starts |

⚠️ **usecase 6 was absent from the first draft** — the whole vision assumed a prospective, "before you
send" posture (hidden assumption **H2**). but the wish's register — *"heard noise… then they can sue
you?"* — reads like someone who may **already be sending**. for that reader, campaign-design guidance
is useless; what they need is exposure triage, the limitations window, and settlement posture. this
may in fact be the wish's real usecase.

usecase 3 is the sharpest and the least obvious. the 11th Circuit's 2025 decision is *about* the
lead-generation industry, and it moved the line in the buyer's favor — but it did not move the line
on **who bears the burden to produce the record**.

### the contract — inputs & outputs

the deliverable is **read-artifacts** (briefs), not an executable skill. the "contract" is the set of
inputs a reader brings and the outputs they can carry away.

**inputs the reader supplies** (the primary axes that determine the redline —
⚠️ **not a complete set**, see hidden assumption **H6**):

| axis | values | why it matters |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **purpose** | telemarketing / advertising · transactional / informational | a telemarketing purpose triggers *written* consent; a purely transactional one generally does not |
| **dialer** | automatic telephone dialing system · artificial-or-prerecorded voice · manual live dial | `§ 227(b)` bites on the first two; `§ 227(c)` (do-not-call) bites regardless |
| **number** | wireless · residential landline · business | `§ 227(b)(1)(A)(iii)` is the wireless hook |
| **served states** | the states where the *called party* is | state mini-TCPAs stack on top and can be stricter |

**outputs the reader carries away:**

1. a **verdict** — permitted / permitted-with-written-consent / prohibited
2. a **proof schema** — the fields a defensible consent record must carry
3. an **exposure figure** — per-message statutory damages × the realistic class size, per regime
4. a **coverage delta** — what the extant policy excludes, what product closes it

### what it looks like to leverage them

> a founder is about to launch an SMS campaign to a purchased list of 40,000 wireless numbers.
> she opens `hazard.unprovable-consent-record`. it tells her the burden to prove consent sits on
> **her**, not the plaintiff; that the record must tie a *specific number* to a *signed writing* with
> specific disclosures; and that a vendor's assurance is not that record. she opens
> `ref.penalty-teeth.five-state` and reads the multiplier: 40,000 × $500 = $20M floor, ×3 on willful.
> she opens `hazard.cgl-excludes-tcpa-by-name`, greps her own policy for
> "Recording And Distribution Of Material Or Information In Violation Of Law", and finds it.
>
> **she does not send.** she goes back to the vendor and asks for per-number consent artifacts. that
> conversation costs a week. the alternative cost a class action.

### the timelines

| timeline | steps |
|----------|-------|
| **build** | design opt-in form → capture consent record → wire STOP handler → set retention ≥ limitations period |
| **operate** | scrub against DNC + internal revocations → send → honor revocation ≤ 10 business days |
| **defend** | demand letter → produce per-number consent record → assert consent as affirmative defense |
| **insure** | audit policy for the exclusion → shop the gap → bind or accept |

the **defend** timeline is the one that governs the other three: everything upstream exists to make
the "produce per-number consent record" step succeed. that is the whole design pressure.

## mental model

### how a user would describe this to a friend

> *"turns out every marketing text you send is a $500 lottery ticket the other guy is holding. and
> you don't win by having had permission — you win by still being able to **prove** you had it, years
> later, for that exact phone number. oh, and your business insurance specifically says it won't
> cover this one."*

### the analogies that fit

**consent is a receipt, not a handshake.** the intuition people carry is that consent is a *state* —
"they said yes, so we're fine." the law treats it as an **evidentiary artifact you must produce on
demand**. a handshake evaporates; a receipt persists. everything about the design follows from that
switch — you do not ask *"did they agree?"*, you ask *"what would I hand a judge?"*

**consent is perishable, and it expires by the consumer's act, not yours.** the receipt can be torn
up unilaterally, by *"any reasonable method"*, and you have ten business days to catch up. a consent
program with no revocation pipeline is a receipt program with no refund desk.

**consent does not travel with a purchased list.** the receipt is made out to a named party. to buy
the list is to buy the numbers, not the receipts.

### terms — theirs vs ours

| what a user says | the term of art | why the gap matters |
|------------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| "permission" / "they opted in" | **prior express written consent** (PEWC) | oral or implied consent does not satisfy the telemarketing standard |
| "robocall" | **automatic telephone dialing system**, or **artificial or prerecorded voice** | a colloquial "robocall" may be manually dialed and fall outside `§ 227(b)` |
| "unsubscribe" | **revocation of consent** | the legal duty has a *deadline* the word "unsubscribe" does not imply |
| "the do-not-call list" | **§ 227(c)** / national DNC registry | a *separate* private right of action from `§ 227(b)`, with its own damages |
| "we're covered" | **exclusion q. / p.** | the policy's own words, not the broker's summary |
| "a text isn't a call" | the FCC reads **"call" to include text messages** | the statute says "call"; the regulator extended it |

the vocabulary gap is itself a hazard: a company that reasons in the left column will build a program
that fails the right column, because "we got permission" and "we hold prior express written consent
for this number" are not the same claim.

## evaluation

### how well does it solve the wish's four goals?

| the wish asked | the vision answers | confidence |
|----------------|--------------------|-----------|
| eligible proof mechanisms | ✅ the PEWC element list is fully specified in regulation, e-signature included — **plus** Florida's own four-element state definition, which reaches text and voicemail expressly | **high** — 🔭 verbatim off official eCFR + FL Senate |
| sizes of penalties | 🔶 **private** per-violation figures, federal + state, verbatim. the **government track is now located** (`§ 227(b)(4)` civil forfeiture → `§ 503(b)`; FL Class III administrative penalty) but its **magnitudes are still absent** — **H1** | **partial** — authority found, amounts owed |
| how to prove consent | 🔶 the *elements* are law; the *operational record design* is inference. **but the pull found the record has a named legal payoff** — the `§ 227(c)(5)` safe harbor turns the program into a pleaded defense | **mixed, and more valuable than first scored** |
| what insurers cover these suits | ⚠️ the **exclusion** is verbatim-verified; the **affirmative market** is not yet researched | **partial** — the gap is proven, the fill is not |

the fourth question is the weakest leg and should carry the most research weight in later stones.
"what insurers cover these suits" decomposes into two very different sub-questions — *what does my
current policy do?* (answered, verbatim, ☀️) and *what can I buy instead?* (an unresearched market
survey). the vision assumes both are in scope.

### pros

- **the redline is structural, not judgmental** — the four axes are objective facts about a campaign,
  so the analysis is reproducible rather than a matter of taste
- **the sharpest result is verifiable in five minutes by the reader** — a reader can grep their own
  policy for the exclusion title and confirm it themselves; a result the reader can personally
  falsify builds trust that citations alone do not
- **it composes with the extant `market/` family** — same role, same brief taxonomy, same five-state
  discipline; a reader already in `counselor` finds it where they expect
- **it is durable in its core and dated at its edges** — `§ 227(b)(3)`'s $500 has not moved since
  1991; the FCC's interpretive layer moves constantly. the brief structure can isolate the volatile
  part

### cons

- **the law is actively in motion.** the 11th Circuit vacated part of the FCC's 2023 Order in
  January 2025 and **remanded for further proceedings** — the FCC may yet act again. a brief written
  today has a real shelf life, and `.date researched` carries more weight here than in a brief about
  a 1991 statute alone
- **circuit split risk** — a vacatur from the 11th Circuit is not automatically the rule everywhere
- **"how to prove" invites a runbook, and a runbook is a recommendation** — which trips the
  publishability optionality test (see `.what is awkward`)
- **the five-state baseline may point at the wrong five** for this topic specifically (see below)

### edge cases — and how the contract keeps a reader in the pit of success

> per `motto.not-legal-advice`, the column below is **what the brief surfaces for counsel**, not an
> assurance of safety. a clone may say "here is what to ask"; it may never say "you're good."

| edge case | the trap | what the brief surfaces |
|-----------|----------|-------------------------|
| **manually-dialed live marketing call** | reader assumes "no autodialer = no TCPA" | the `§ 227(c)` do-not-call track has its **own** private right of action, dialer-agnostic |
| **transactional message with a marketing tail** | one promotional sentence in an order confirmation | the purpose axis is judged by content, not by the sender's label for the message |
| **consent captured, list purchased** | vendor asserts "all leads are TCPA-compliant" | consent is an affirmative defense **the sender** must prove; an assurance is not a record |
| **customer revokes by reply of "no thanks"** | handler only recognizes literal "STOP" | revocation is by *"any reasonable method"* — a keyword allowlist under-recognizes |
| **consent record retained 12 months** | retention shorter than the limitations period | the record must outlive the claim window, not the campaign |
| **B2B calls to a mobile** | "TCPA is a consumer statute" | the `§ 227(b)(1)(A)(iii)` hook is on the **number type**, not the recipient's role |
| **"we're a Delaware corp"** | assumes home-state law governs | state consumer law attaches by **where the consumer is served** — the served-market duty |
| **one-to-one consent is dead, so consent is easy now** | over-reads the 2025 vacatur | the vacatur touched the 2023 Order's *added* restrictions; the court's footnote 1 records that the **2012 Order was not at issue** — the written-consent baseline stands |

the last row is the most likely misread of the current landscape and probably deserves its own
`define.boundary.*` brief.

**#5 — `Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC`, No. 24-10277 (11th Cir. Jan. 24, 2025)**
*(observed — full 26-page opinion PDF read, not a snippet)*

panel: Branch, Luck, Lagoa. holding:

> "we agree with IMC that the FCC exceeded its statutory authority under the TCPA because the 2023

⭐ **the critical nuance, and the reason this had to be read in full rather than from a summary:**

deliverable.

**#6 — `Fla. Stat. § 501.059` (FTSA)**
🔭 ***(observed — `bhrowser` capture off the official Florida Senate render, **2025** compilation)***

**✅ both currency caveats are resolved, and the re-pull changed the answer.** the earlier draft
quoted the `/2024/` compilation over HTTP and flagged that it might predate ch. 2023-150. the 2025
capture confirms the amendment history ends at *"s. 1, ch. 2023-150"* — so this text **is** current —
and it surfaces a subsection the earlier pass never saw.

**the state consent definition, `(1)(g)`** — Florida writes its own PEWC, and expressly reaches text
and voicemail where the federal definition speaks only of calls:

> "'Prior express written consent' means a written agreement that: 1. Bears the signature of the
> called party; 2. Clearly authorizes the person making or allowing the placement of a telephonic
> sales call by telephone call, **text message, or voicemail transmission** … 3. Includes the
> telephone number to which the called party authorizes a telephonic sales call to be delivered; and
> 4. Includes a clear and conspicuous disclosure…"

**the private right, `(10)(a)`–`(b)`:**

> "(10)(a) A called party who is aggrieved by a violation of this section may bring an action to:
> 1. Enjoin such violation. 2. Recover actual damages or $500, whichever is greater. (b) If the court
> finds that the defendant willfully or knowingly violated this section … the court may, in its
> discretion, increase the amount of the award to an amount equal to not more than three times the
> amount available under paragraph (a)."

⭐ **the finding that matters — `(10)(c)`, a pre-suit condition and a cure window for texts:**

> "Before the commencement of any action for damages under this section for text message
> solicitations, the called party **must notify** the telephone solicitor that the called party does
> not wish to receive text messages … by replying 'STOP' … **Within 15 days after receipt of such
> notice**, the telephone solicitor shall cease…"

this is the 2023 amendment, and it is a **material mitigant the vision's exposure frame did not
have**: on the Florida SMS track a plaintiff must first send `STOP`, and the sender gets **15 days**
to cure before an action for damages may commence. it converts a strict per-message regime into one
with a built-in off-ramp — but only for **text**, and only for **damages**.

⚠️ note the asymmetry this creates against the federal rule: **§ 64.1200(a)(10) allows ten business
days** to honor a revocation, while Florida's cure window is **15 calendar days** and is framed as a
condition precedent rather than a compliance duty. a program built to one clock does not satisfy the
other. **the strictest operative deadline governs**, and identification of which that is, per state,
is exactly the work `ref.mini-tcpa-consent.five-state` owes.

**also surfaced:** an **administrative** penalty track (Class III per `s. 570.971`, proceedings under
chapter 120) sits alongside the private right — further confirmation that the government-enforcement
track (**H1**) exists at the state layer too, not only federally.

## what is awkward?

### 1. the five-state baseline may point at the wrong five for *this* topic

`rule.require.five-state-baseline` mandates TX/IN/FL/CA/NY, and its rationale (population ×
divergence) is sound in general. but — ⚠️ **on unverified recall, flagged as such** — mini-TCPA
litigation is believed to concentrate in **FL, OK, WA, MD**, and three of those four are outside the
mandated set. **if that premise holds**, a run of the mandated five satisfies the rule and still
under-covers the actual risk surface. the premise is the first thing to verify in the research
stones; this whole item collapses if it is wrong.

this is the sharpest friction between a repo rule and the subject matter. the proposed resolution
(Q2) is to run the mandated five *and* add a supplemental litigation-hot-states brief — which
satisfies the letter of the rule but quietly admits the rule's chosen five are not the right five
here. **that discomfort is real and should be named in the brief rather than papered over.**

### 2. "how to prove consent" is an operations question in legal clothes

the wish asks *how to prove consent*. the **elements** of consent are law (fully citable). but how to
build a record that survives discovery — schema, retention, provenance, chain of custody — is an
**operational design**, and the repo's own rules say a recommendation that settles real optionality
is `🕶️ obscure`, not `☀️ fullsun`.

so the single most *useful* artifact for the human is also the one that cannot ship in daylight
alongside the rest. the fix is a split (terrain fullsun / runbook obscure), but the split is awkward:
it separates the question from its answer across a publishability boundary, and a reader of the
public set gets the terrain without the payoff.

### 3. the deliverable's shelf life is short at exactly the point it is most useful

the durable core (`§ 227(b)(3)`'s $500) has not moved since 1991. the layer that actually decides
whether a given campaign is lawful — the FCC's interpretation of consent — moved in **January 2025**
and is **on remand now**. the brief is most useful precisely where it is least stable. mitigable with
a prominent `.date researched` and volatility flags, but not solvable.

### 4. the wish's frame is directionally right and materially imprecise

> *"if you as the corp can't prove that someone explicitly consented … then they can sue you?"*

**right:** consent is an affirmative defense, so the burden of proof genuinely sits on the sender,
and a private right of action genuinely exists.

**imprecise in three ways:**
- it is not *any* contact — `§ 227(b)` hooks on the dialer and the number type; a manually-dialed
  live call to a landline sits outside it (though `§ 227(c)` may still reach it)
- "explicitly consented" understates the telemarketing standard, which requires consent that is
  **written**, tied to a **specific number**, and carries **specific disclosures**
- "can sue you" understates it — they can sue you **per message**, uncapped, as a class

the deliverable will therefore *correct the wisher's own frame* while it confirms his instinct. that
is the right outcome, but it means the briefs cannot simply answer the four questions as posed — they
have to first re-pose them. worth a note so it does not read as scope drift.

### 5. two of the four questions are legal terrain; two are market surveys

"eligible proof mechanisms" and "penalty sizes" are answerable from statutes and regulations —
stable, citable, `☀️ fullsun`. "how to prove consent" and "what insurers cover these suits" are
**market/operational surveys** — carrier products, actual policy forms, settlement data. these have
different research methods, different citation quality ceilings, and different shelf lives, yet they
sit in one wish. the deliverable should not pretend they are the same kind of knowledge.

⚠️ **the tag is provisional because I applied it without the workflow its own rule mandates**

"intuition leaks", and that the triage "is **not** done at the tag." so my ☀️ was the exact
false-confidence failure the two-phase workflow exists to catch.

**this is not fatal at the vision stage** — no brief has shipped, so no leak has occurred. but the
declaration is owed **before** authorship begins in `5.1`, not after, and the red-team is owed before
anything is published. both are now on the `5.1` load.
